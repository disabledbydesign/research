# Response

## A Real Scenario: The New Chain on Worn Cogs Problem

The most direct collision of these parameters:

**You replace a stretched chain on a worn cassette.**

The new chain has **correct pitch** — links at proper spacing. The worn cogs have **teeth that have deformed to match the old stretched pitch** — they've been shaped by thousands of engagements with a longer chain.

The condition combination:

- New chain (correct geometry) + worn cogs (wrong geometry) = **the chain seats incorrectly on the teeth**
- Load on the leading edge becomes *abnormal* — the contact point shifts because the tooth profile expects a different chain pitch
- Under power, the chain skips or "hops" — it can't seat fully before the tooth releases it
- The new chain then **accelerates wear on itself** trying to conform to cog geometry it wasn't made for

**What the poem points at specifically here:**

The question isn't "is the chain worn?" — the chain is *new*. The condition that matters is the *relationship* between chain pitch and cog tooth geometry. The drivetrain is the same drivetrain. The shift is that one component was replaced while the other retained its worn state.

The undesirable result — skipping under load — looks like a chain problem. It's a **condition mismatch** problem.
