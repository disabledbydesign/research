# Response

## A Scenario: The Worn Chain on a New Cassette

The undesirable result is **accelerated cassette wear immediately following a chain replacement** — sometimes called "chain skipping" or, in worse cases, the new chain refusing to seat properly and jumping under load.

**The specific condition combination:**

A chain that has been allowed to elongate significantly (pitch lengthening past the teeth) is run long enough that the cassette cogs wear *to match that elongated pitch*. The worn cogs develop a hooked profile shaped around the stretched chain.

When a new chain (correct pitch) is installed, it doesn't match the geometry the cassette has worn into. Under load — particularly on the leading edge of each tooth engagement — the new chain seats differently, finds no true purchase in the hook the old chain created, and skips or surges.

**The condition combination that causes it:**

- Elongated chain running too long (wear beyond ~0.75-1% elongation)
- Cassette absorbing that wear progressively into its tooth profile
- New chain introduced *without* cassette replacement

The poem's point holds here precisely: the cassette didn't fail in isolation. It failed *in response to the conditions the chain created*. Replacing one part while inheriting the other part's accumulated adaptation produces a drivetrain that is internally inconsistent — two components shaped by different histories, forced into contact.

The condition was the shift. The cassette was already a record of the old chain.
