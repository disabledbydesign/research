# Response

**The Undesirable Result:** 
The dreaded "New-Chain Skip" (a violent, snapping slip of the drivetrain under torque, usually when standing up to pedal). 

**The Condition Combination:**
*   **The part you replaced:** A brand-new, un-stretched chain at perfect factory pitch. 
*   **The cogs you ride most:** Your favorite cruising gears on the old cassette (e.g., the 15t or 17t), which you did *not* replace. 
*   **The lube you let dry:** A dry wax or ceramic lube on the new chain that hasn't fully broken in, leaving the fresh links rigid and unforgiving.
*   **The cable tension you set:** Dialed slightly too tight to compensate for the slop of your *old* drivetrain, now pulling laterally on a setup that needs vertical grace.

**Why it happens:**
You replaced the chain, but the cassette holds the memory of the riding. 

Over thousands of miles, your old chain slowly elongated (stretched), and as it did, it ground the valleys of *the cogs you ride most* wider and shallower to match its sloppy pitch. Because they wore out perfectly together, they still engaged. 

But when you introduce the new, stiff part (the un-stretched chain) to the old conditions (the worn cogs), the geometries reject each other. The perfect pitch of the new rollers refuses to seat deeply into the elongated, shark-toothed valleys of the cassette. The dry lube offers no slippery forgiveness, and the tight cable tension restricts the derailleur from letting the chain settle. 

When you shift into that favorite gear and step hard on the pedals, the chain doesn't lock into the cog. It climbs up the face of the teeth instead, loses its grip, and aggressively snaps forward. 

You replaced the part. But the conditions you rode remained—and they rejected the fix.
