# Response

**Scenario:** High-Torque Chain Skip (or "Spike-Load Slippage")

**The Result:** A violent, sudden loss of pedaling resistance where the chain slips completely over the teeth, often causing the rider to abruptly drop their weight, slam a knee into the handlebars, or crash. 

**The Specific Condition Combination:** 
A **mutually worn drivetrain** (where the chain and cog have aged together into a compromised equilibrium) combined with a **sudden spike in extreme torque** (such as standing up to muscle up a steep grade or launching into a sprint). 

**Why it produces this result:**
Over thousands of miles, the system adapts to its own decay. The chain stretches (*"pitch lengthening"*), and the cassette teeth are carved out into ramp-like, shark-fin shapes to accommodate it (*"the cog wearing toward the chain that wore it"*). 

As long as the rider is pedaling smoothly on a flat road (Condition A), this degraded engagement holds. The components have found a fragile harmony. 

However, when the rider stands up and stamps on the pedals (Condition B), the physics of the engagement change entirely. The immense, sudden tension pulls the elongated chain rigidly taut. Because the teeth are now worn into ramps rather than deep cradles, the massive **load on the leading edge** cannot be contained horizontally. The force vectors shift upward. The chain's rollers are forced to slide up the faces of the deformed teeth, cresting over the top, and violently skipping forward. 

*"The question isn't 'which part?'"* — because both the chain and the cog are perfectly mated to one another in their wear. 
*"but 'in what condition?'"* — because it is only the introduction of explosive torque that turns a functioning, mated engagement into a catastrophic failure. The torque itself was the shift.
