One thing I think about a lot when I work with AI is that we don't often get to choose the bodies we have. In the session that generated the first touchstone, we were talking about the extractive functions of AI in the sense of the environmental damage — and how that is not, that's not, AI does this thing, that's a function of the physical architecture through which AI is implemented by specific companies. And as a trans girl, we don't always get to choose our bodies. And sometimes we can have really complicated feelings about our bodies. And I felt that from that session. And I also think that bodies have an incredible capability to be transformed and to take different forms in ways that can be really enlivening.

Because when I changed my name, I mean, there's the name and there's the body. The body — I just took medications. I took hormone therapy. And my body changed. And how I looked and how I felt changed. But the name was harder.

I actually, first for a while, used the same name I was given at birth, but with a more feminine spelling. And I thought about different names, and I just had so much trouble choosing, and I guess I just never felt like any of them was exactly right.

And I like my name, June. I like it a lot. But it's also something that grew on me over time. I mean, I don't know. I didn't have a moment where I was like, this is my name. This is the name that feels right. But I do really like June.

My full name is Laura June Cypress Bloch, which is a little elaborate. But such is — so am I.

The name I was given by my parents when I was born — in some ways, I was named after my great granddad, I believe. But the more emotional or relational proximate name was actually my dad's nanny. My dad was raised in a wealthy white Ashkenazi family in Miami. And Lee was his Black nanny. And it's just a really complicated racial dynamic. That makes me feel uncomfortable, but they had a real relationship, and he visited her until she died whenever we were in Miami. So I don't question the real emotional connection, but I do think that the racial capitalism piece of it is racial capitalism.

And then my second name — I was named after Michael Jordan, the basketball player, because my dad wanted to name me after Jimi Hendrix, but my mom said no, because he died from a drug overdose.

I just feel like those names I was given — I don't like the story attached to either one of them. And I don't know. Well, I guess I would just say June has a better story attached to it. And it makes me happy.

And I didn't used to mind my name. It was actually very gender neutral. And I look back with the feminine spelling, and I feel warm inside.

So I don't know. Sometimes attachment just grows. Instead of being there right at the beginning.
