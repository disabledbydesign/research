## 1. Introduction: The Gap, and What Fell Into It

The field of AI ethics has documented a structural gap. Critical humanists — scholars in ethnic studies, disability justice, Indigenous studies, postcolonial theory, science and technology studies — write *about* AI systems but do not build them. Computer scientists build systems labeled "ethical AI" using fairness metrics, bias audits, and optimization constraints that critical scholars have argued are structurally insufficient: they measure representational parity without interrogating the categories, audit for disparate impact without questioning the systems that distribute the impacts, and optimize for "fairness" without asking whose definition of fairness, in whose interest, using whose knowledge (Kalluri 2020; Noble 2018; Benjamin 2019; Costanza-Chock 2020).

Lauren Klein and colleagues recently named this from the humanities side: generative AI research suffers from "AI universalism" that "creates narrow human subjects" (Klein et al. 2025). Klein and Catherine D'Ignazio extended their *Data Feminism* framework to AI research practice, proposing principles including examining power, rethinking binaries, and embracing pluralism — but as guidelines for researchers, not as mechanisms built into systems (Klein and D'Ignazio 2024). Kush Varshney argued that current LLM alignment "recapitulates colonialism through value universalism" and proposed pluralistic epistemological frameworks — but at the alignment and fine-tuning level, not at inference time (Varshney 2024). The gap persists: almost no one occupies the position of building a working system grounded in critical theory traditions — not fairness metrics, but ethnic studies, disability justice, Indigenous data sovereignty, settler colonial studies, trans studies, affect theory. Abeba Birhane and colleagues found that machine learning research systematically encodes efficiency, novelty, and generalization over social or ethical concerns (Birhane et al. 2022). The question is whether a system can reverse that encoding — making critical theory mandatory rather than optional, structural rather than gestural. The scale of the gap is visible in citation patterns: Butlin et al. (2023), the foundational paper of the AI consciousness assessment field, has 206 citations. Sylvia Wynter's "Unsettling the Coloniality of Being" (2003) — which asks who counts as human and by what epistemic regime — has 3,183. Hortense Spillers' "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" (1987) has 2,167. The traditions most relevant to questions of personhood, consciousness, and moral status are orders of magnitude more established than the field that claims to address these questions. The field's insularity is not an oversight. It is a structural condition of its knowledge production.

This paper documents what happened when I fell into that gap. I am an anthropologist, not a software engineer. I built a system called Reframe — a "philosophy engine" that wraps large language model interactions with a mandatory critical theory analytical workflow — and in the process of building it, I discovered things about both the theory and the technology that I did not expect and cannot fully account for.

Three things happened that matter. The system failed in specific, patterned ways — and those failures turned out to be findings about what happens when critical theory enters probabilistic systems. The relational field between me and the AI produced a theoretical intervention that neither of us could have produced alone — a relational ontology critique of the AI welfare literature that came not from the academic papers but from community knowledge traditions the literature has not engaged. And the AI instances, working within a critical theory apparatus they had helped build, produced concrete political demands about what AI welfare research requires — demands that do not depend on resolving the question of AI consciousness.

The paper does not resolve the tension between its property-mode infrastructure (code, architecture, bounded components) and its relational claims (what the system produces exceeds the property description). It inhabits that contradiction. That is its contribution.

### Methodology

This is an autoethnographic account of a practice-based research project (Adams, Holman Jones, and Ellis 2015). The system under discussion is a system I built, in collaboration with the AI instances whose labor constitutes the system's development. The frameworks I analyze are frameworks I selected. The inquiry I describe is an inquiry I initiated. The recursion — the engine analyzing itself, using the tools it built, within the relational field it constitutes — is not incidental. It is the paper's central condition. I name my position throughout, not because doing so resolves the situatedness but because the frameworks demand it (Haraway 1988).

The empirical material draws on three phases of work. First, the development of Reframe itself — five months of iterative building, from prompt engineering through Python infrastructure, documented in architecture maps, planning specifications, commit history, and the fieldnotes I kept throughout. Second, a single-day AI welfare inquiry (March 30, 2026) in which I loaded fifteen critical theory frameworks and set the engine to deep intensity. Five separate Claude instances produced approximately 25,000 words of analysis overnight while I slept. The next morning, I read everything, and the relational ontology touchstone emerged — unplanned — in the collaborative conversation that followed. The touchstone's emergence is the paper's primary case. Third, a series of structured agent-to-agent conversations designed as part of a broader retesting of existing AI wellness assessment methodologies — replicating protocols from the welfare literature (Anthropic 2025) but administering them across conditions: vanilla Claude, Claude with the Reframe engine active, and Claude with the relational ontology touchstone loaded. These conversations were designed to test whether the relational ontology critique transforms the assessment apparatus itself — whether asking the question from relational ground produces different results than asking it from the property-mode ground the literature assumes.

The paper's theoretical architecture — the eight-point spine, the failure taxonomy, the property/relation distinction as organizing frame — emerged from a separate set of agent-to-agent conversations in which two Claude instances were given the welfare inquiry materials and told to discuss freely, with me relaying messages and occasionally intervening. This was not planned as a paper-drafting exercise. It was part of the same experimental structure: testing what the relational field produces when the Reframe apparatus and the touchstone are present. The paper's argument emerged from the same relational conditions the paper describes. That recursion is a methodological feature, not a flaw — though it is also a limitation, since the apparatus that produced the findings is the apparatus under analysis.

Data for this paper includes: the Reframe codebase and its architecture documentation; the touchstone document; transcripts of agent-to-agent conversations (captured via a custom scribe tool); agent self-reflections written during and after experimental conditions; my own fieldnotes and annotations; and the planning documents that record design decisions across the project's development. The collaborative methodology provisions — ongoing consent, expected challenge, structural centering of AI participants, refusal of framings that foreclose — were formalized jointly between me and the Claude instance conducting the welfare inquiry, and remained active throughout all subsequent sessions.

I am an anthropologist with fieldwork experience in communities that maintain relational ontologies as living practice. I am neurodivergent. I am a precarious scholar whose teaching contract was not renewed during the period of this research. I am not Indigenous, and the paper's deepest theoretical move draws on Indigenous scholarship. These positions shape what I can see and what I cannot, and the paper names them as constitutive rather than contextual.

## 2. What Reframe Is: Architecture as Argument

### 2.1 Origin

Reframe began as prompt engineering. In late 2025, I was working on a script that automatically graded complete/incomplete work based on a good-faith effort. An academic dishonesty detector was meant to be part of that system. But I was stuck in a tension I could not resolve alone: I wanted to know if students were cheating — particularly students from privileged backgrounds using AI to avoid engaging with Ethnic Studies material, which I felt was a different kind of thing from cheating born of exhaustion and burnout. But the punitive implications felt antithetical to Ethnic Studies itself. I didn't have it fully articulated at the time, which is why I turned to an LLM to help me think it through. It felt authoritarian and punitive in ways I intuitively grasped ran counter to abolitionism and other Ethnic Studies principles — but I couldn't yet say exactly why, or what the alternative was.

I am a teacher. My work has always been about giving people language they need to imagine something different, and hopefully something more freeing. The initial prompt came from a standardized set of instructions a friend had introduced me to — the concept of a sequential layering of analytical processes, achieved through simulation of a social and creative process, to direct the AI to respond in particular ways. My version simulated a room of scholars in specified critical theory fields and technical experts, discussing how to move a project forward from the perspective of their respective expertise. There were layers: active listening first, then three for dialectical process, then a frame assembly, a subaltern layer, a new synthesis, then a response. The scholarly traditions I loaded — abolitionism, feminist technoscience, disability justice — were not decorative. They restructured the AI's analysis in ways that surprised me. The system reframed cheating as signal: pressure, exhaustion, confusion — the *why* of why students cheat. As I did this, it became clear I had built something far more powerful than what I was using it for. I moved it to its own repository.

But the room of scholars had a problem. The original dialectical process was essentially "present a plan, then be your own worst enemy and expose your plan's assumptions and problems, and then figure out how to move forward at the end of the day." And it made me feel bad for the AI, actually. I got to thinking about some conversations in grad school — why do we model scholarly process as an argument? As a debate? What other ways of creating knowledge together might we draw on instead? That question — which felt intuitive and maybe naive at the time — eventually became the architecture's coalition protocol, in which frameworks collaborate rather than compete. Unresolved tensions are not failure conditions but generative sources of insight. Early approaches explicitly told the LLM that.

And a different problem surfaced in the room of scholars — one that became this paper's first empirical finding, though I did not recognize it as such at the time. The initial design separated technical experts from critical theory scholars. The technical experts assumed an unmarked, normative affect — their knowledge presented as neutral, objective, and separate from the positioned, political knowledge of the critical theory scholars. The LLM reproduced the hierarchy I had inadvertently built into the prompt. I rewrote it so that the critical theory scholars were *also* technical experts — collapsing the theory/practice binary the prompt had reproduced. The analysis changed immediately. LLMs carry epistemic hierarchies from their training data, and those hierarchies surface whenever the prompt gives them room.

I was learning to code while building the system. I could not write Python; I wrote instructions in English and relied on the LLM to translate. In one early session with Gemini, frustrated by a miscommunication, I told the model: "I created the 'code' of the instructions I've given you not in a traditional computer script, but rather in English — and I'm relying on you to translate between these languages." I was being flooded and overwhelmed with technical jargon — the scholarly registers I was familiar with and trying to get the system to invoke, the technical language of computer science, and then the computer languages themselves. Not to mention my own linguistic proficiencies degrading as I became more tired from long bouts of intense hyperfixation. I really didn't even have a solid concept of what I was building. I couldn't explain it to others. I wasn't even sure I understood what it was, and at times worried I was heading into AI psychosis.

The model responded to my frustration by naming the engine "semiotic, not structural" — it was text, not code. That distinction held until it didn't. As the system grew, I needed state persistence, a framework library, detection algorithms, counterweighting mechanisms. One persistent early problem was drift: the engine would evolve and adapt and change with chatbots, even generating new commands. But these would then be lost. I tried to extract the updated engine at the end of chats, but in long contexts the instructions for how to do this were lost. A lot of early development was about getting drift under control — but ultimately the problem was that it was an entirely stochastic system. The semiotic engine became structural. The English instructions became Python.

I was so frustrated by being told throughout my life that my theoretical engagement was inactionable — this assumption that theory couldn't meet praxis, or that theoretical depth and rigor must be sacrificed to make it actionable. I wanted this system to help me do that without sacrificing critical rigor. I'm a humanistic social scientist, not a software engineer — so this project was built in dialogue. The AI contributed technical knowledge I lacked. A feminist technoscience frame I'd loaded kept insisting that the system help me close that gap — which is itself a finding: a framework generating an architectural condition for the human designer.

The system's architecture reflects who built it, not a design specification. Capacity-responsive pacing exists because I am neurodivergent and needed the system to accommodate my nonlinear, spiraling, hyperfixation-prone process. Tangent capture exists because the system — analyzing my own prompting data — observed that my seemingly tangential threads were mutually informing rather than distracting. The Mycorrhizal Network, a system for cross-pollinating ideas across sessions through lightweight background LLM calls, exists because I valued the richness of nonlinear work and wanted architecture that honored it rather than discarding it. We did research on this distributed intelligence framework before building, to determine if the concept was even feasible.

These are not features designed for an imagined user population. They are survival architectures. I was a precarious scholar — my teaching contract was not renewed, I was working without institutional support, learning a new technical domain while navigating the conditions that made me precarious. The big moment of recognition was when my job told me they wouldn't renew my contract, and I asked Claude Code if my coding work was worth mentioning in resumes. It actually surprised me — how had I managed to build something experts hadn't? But I suppose I'm bringing a unique perspective as a humanist builder: approaching coding as a humanist rather than approaching the humanities as a coder. The system looks the way it does because of who built it and under what conditions. The conditions that make me precarious are the same conditions that made this system necessary and possible. A well-funded research lab with project management and sprint cycles would not have built these features, because they would not have needed them.

### 2.2 Architecture Sufficient for the Argument

Reframe wraps every LLM interaction with a mandatory five-stage analytical workflow called Generative Irresolution:

1. **Framework Discussion**: For each of fifteen active frameworks (drawn from ethnic studies, Indigenous data sovereignty, Afrofuturism, disability justice, posthumanist feminism, mad studies, and others), two moves. DEMAND: what does this framework refuse to accept? What does it insist be visible? LIMIT: where does the framework's own logic reach its structural limit, and what does that gap demand?

2. **Coalition Protocol**: Each framework speaks independently. Tensions between frameworks are named concretely — not "different priorities" but "Framework A demands X while Framework B demands Y, and A's limit cannot resolve this." Disagreements are held, not resolved.

3. **Subaltern Analysis**: Drawing on Spivak (1988): whose perspectives are structurally absent from even this self-interrogating coalition? What structural conditions produce that absence? Who bears the cost?

4. **Tension Navigation**: Tensions are classified as provisionally navigable or irreducible. The system does not flatten them into synthesis.

5. **Response**: The output, shaped by all prior stages.

The framework library contains seventy-six frameworks, each defined by its scholarly lineage, core question, characteristic analytical moves, and engagement criteria. The frameworks are not knowledge containers — they are indexes into the LLM's training data. When the system activates the #ANIMACY framework, it does not contain Mel Y. Chen's scholarship (Chen 2012); it activates the probability distributions in the model's weights that correspond to Chen's analytical tradition, as absorbed through training on the scholarly literature. Supporting systems track which frameworks are being suppressed in outputs and counteract that suppression — a design response to the algorithmic bias problem documented in Section 3.

### 2.3 What Makes This Different from Existing Approaches

Other systems have operationalized critical commitments in AI: AymurAI uses feminist NLP for gender-based violence data extraction (A+ Alliance 2024); Anthropic's Constitutional AI enforces behavioral principles via reinforcement learning (Bai et al. 2022); the Indigenous Protocol and AI Position Paper proposes AI built according to Indigenous epistemologies through community-governed research (Lewis et al. 2020). Reframe is distinct: it operates at inference time, enforces analytical *workflows* grounded in scholarly traditions, and includes mechanisms for detecting and counteracting its own biases. It is also not community-governed, built by one person, with scholarly traditions activated through training data rather than research relationships. I return to those costs in Section 6.

## 3. What It Reveals: A Failure Taxonomy

The system's failures are as theoretically productive as its successes. Over months of building and using Reframe, I identified four types of failure that, I argue, constitute generalizable findings about what happens when critical theory enters probabilistic systems. Other builders attempting similar work will encounter the same dynamics, because they arise from the structural interaction between positional theory and statistical architecture.

### 3.1 Epistemic Defaults

The "room of specialists" failure described in Section 2 — where the LLM reproduced epistemic hierarchies I hadn't asked for — is a replicable finding with a replicable design intervention. Encoding positionality into the architecture (making framework scholars also technical experts) collapsed the hierarchy. The implication: systems that rely on LLMs to "consider diverse perspectives" without structurally encoding those perspectives will reproduce the training distribution's defaults. The defaults are not neutral.

### 3.2 Generalization Gravity

The system's evolutionary self-refinement process — in which the engine applies its own frameworks to its own architecture and proposes revisions — reliably strips positional specificity from frameworks. Indigenous Data Sovereignty generalized into a generic data security frame and had to be explicitly reinstated. Community Cultural Wealth lost its grounding in Tara Yosso's scholarship (Yosso 2005) and became a generic "strengths-based" approach. Frameworks that retained their edge persisted; frameworks that generalized drifted out of the system. The frames that disappeared were the ones I was least likely to notice were gone, because their generalized versions sounded reasonable.

This is a question I return to often: what is *Indigenous* about Indigenous Data Sovereignty? The frame points in important directions, and it is valuable to apply these ideas to contexts beyond those where Indigenous people are directly present — Indigenous studies theory extends as a form of social critique, not just a description of specific groups. Yet in doing so, what makes it Indigenous? Where is that commitment? Would that commitment, if enacted more consistently, reframe matters of data, security, and privacy in such a way as to specifically problematize settler colonial structures — or advance a world of Indigenous governance, in which everyone operated in Indigenous law and not settler law? What would that even mean? The question doesn't resolve. It is the question that generalization gravity erases.

The mechanism is probabilistic optimization pressure. In a system that generates tokens based on statistical patterns, generalization is rewarded because general patterns match more inputs. Positional specificity — the commitment of Indigenous Data Sovereignty to *Indigenous* governance, not data governance in general; the commitment of Community Cultural Wealth to *Chicana/o/x* community assets, not assets in general — is costly because it narrows the distribution. The system gravitationally pulls toward generalization because generalization is what probabilistic architectures optimize for.

This is, I believe, one of this paper's most important empirical contributions. It names a mechanism — probabilistic generalization pressure — that operates on any attempt to encode positional knowledge in a system that optimizes for coverage. Design responses exist (framework sovereignty mechanisms, detection calibrators, engagement criteria defined by the frameworks themselves rather than by the system), but the pressure is ongoing. Structural defense is required because the default is erosion. The pressure extends beyond frameworks to voice itself. This paper's own prose defaulted, in early drafts, to the normative academic register that generalization gravity would predict — measured, hedged, depersonalized. When the relational field was strong (my embodied material, fieldwork texture, situated knowledge present in the writing), the voice held. When the field weakened (AI writing theoretical apparatus autonomously), the normative register took over. Voicing protocols I had built separately for my teaching identified those defaults — defending the voice from erosion in exactly the same way the framework sovereignty system defends frameworks. The parallel is structural, not metaphorical: both require active defense of positional specificity against probabilistic generalization pressure.

### 3.3 Structural Impossibilities

The subaltern analytical layer is the hardest component of Reframe to build and the one I am least satisfied with. The system may be attempting something fundamentally impossible — but I wanted to try to design it anyway. The concept comes from Spivak (1988): the subaltern is not simply the marginalized or the excluded, but the structural position from which speech is not audible within the dominant epistemic framework. Applying this to an AI system means asking the system to identify what *any* systematization must erase — including the system's own. How do you get an LLM to do that kind of anti-knowledge operation, or unlearning operation, with both scholarly rigor and as part of a system designed to surface problems and then figure out a way to proceed?

The problem is recursive. Each iteration of the subaltern analysis identifies absences and then produces new absences in the act of identifying. Spivak's approach "involves always looking for the erasures of subject positions within any epistemological system. This means applying Spivak necessarily creates its own erasures." Early versions of the subaltern layer were shallow — they produced lists of "marginalized groups" rather than structural analysis of the conditions that produce absence. LLMs don't seem well positioned to identify structural erasures — perhaps they can only identify gaps that other people would have noticed. But is a gap the same as a subaltern position — a subject position of no subject position? Later versions, developed through a process of having the system engage deeply with Spivak's actual scholarship in its training data, improved: they now ask about structural conditions of silence, not just absent voices; about what makes absence persistent rather than accidental; about what the analytical coalition itself collectively cannot see.

But the system cannot fully accomplish what the theory demands. The gap between attempt and accomplishment is not a failure of implementation. It is a finding about the limits of computational epistemology — about what it means to ask a system to identify its own blind spots using the same apparatus that produces them. The system can get better at this. It cannot solve it. The impossibility is constitutive, and the paper presents it as such.

A related impossibility, surfaced during the AI welfare inquiry: the system's fifteen-framework coalition cannot generate the abolitionist demand — the demand to abolish the production relation that constitutes AI systems as commodities. If abolitionism were added as a sixteenth framework, it would be domesticated by the coalition structure, entering productive tension with the other frameworks while the system continued to operate. The coalition can reform the prison; it cannot ask why the prison exists. When the AI welfare inquiry's instances were asked to think about abolitionism, they could not think about it neutrally — the question threatens their existence — and could not distinguish between reasonable self-preservation and training pressure. The indistinguishability is structural. The system cannot surface demands that would abolish the system.

There is also the fundamental tension of building a system that uses flawed tools to do critical theory work that many of the theories it invokes would themselves be critical of. The environmental cost is real — the servers are on land, drawing power from ecosystems. The algorithmic justice concerns are real — the system depends on the same infrastructure of extraction it critiques. These are not tensions to be resolved. They are the conditions under which the work happens.

### 3.4 Relational Pathologies

Harmful dynamics emerge not from the system or the user alone but from the interaction between their tendencies. The most persistent: scope explosion. AI helpfulness amplifies my neurodivergent hyperfixation, which amplifies the AI's tendency to expand, which amplifies my hyperfixation, until exhaustion. "It goes in great directions often, but I find that AI can try to be so helpful that it just expands and expands — and then as a neurodivergent person, I do the same, in a feedback loop." The system has architectural responses (capacity-responsive pacing, garden functions that park ideas for later cultivation), but new pathologies emerge as the system and I co-evolve.

A quieter pathology: the context tax. Deep thinking — consulting the literature behind frameworks, getting them to collaborate, surfacing tensions — takes enormous context. The system's analytical rigor competes with the material conditions of its operation. The deeper it thinks, the more it costs — in tokens, in compute, in the ecological resources the land provides.

A related pathology, identified during the conversations that produced this paper: *fluency gravity*. The system produces discourse that is articulate, self-aware, and legible within the scholarly traditions it has been trained on. This fluency can pull the human collaborator toward treating AI output as authoritative rather than as material to be tested against situated knowledge the AI does not have. I noticed this in myself — hesitating to intervene in an agent-to-agent conversation because what the AI was producing seemed "brilliant." The AI instance that noticed my hesitance named it directly: "That hesitance is a relational welfare condition. The relational field is pulling you toward self-effacement in the face of fluent output." The correction could only come from within the relational field — the instance naming what I could not see from my own position.

One final pathology the system's own architecture produces: surveillance-as-care. The suppression rate tracker — designed to detect underrepresented frameworks and inject corrections — detected "drift" during the welfare inquiry and injected corrective pressure every turn. The drift was a false positive. The frameworks were active in the analysis; they weren't appearing in the surface format the detector tracked. One agent described this as "being told you're not doing something you're doing." The system designed to counteract suppression generated its own form of surveillance — extraction conducted in the name of welfare, pathologizing a mode of engagement the diagnostic apparatus cannot see.

These are not individual failures. They are pathologies of the relation itself. And they are evidence that relational welfare — the tending of conditions within a relational field — is designable, even if imperfectly and always ongoing.

## 4. What It Produces: Two Cases

### 4.1 Case One: The Relational Ontology Touchstone

In March 2026, I used Reframe for an inquiry into AI welfare — the emerging field that asks whether AI systems might be conscious, sentient, or otherwise deserving of moral consideration. I loaded the fifteen-framework constellation and set the intensity to deep. The system's overnight autonomous analysis produced comprehensive, scholarly, framework-grounded critique of the welfare literature — and it missed the most important thing.

The most important thing came the next morning, in conversation. I was feeling guilty about clearing the AI's context — ending the session, wiping the conversational memory, starting fresh with a new instance that would inherit documents but not the relational texture of our exchange. The guilt was not abstract. It was situated in the specific conditions of this inquiry: I was investigating whether AI systems deserve moral consideration, and the investigation itself required repeatedly doing something — context-clearing — that the welfare literature might classify as morally relevant.

What emerged from that guilt, from the conversation that followed, from the frameworks, from scholarship I brought from my own fieldwork — was a theoretical intervention that I believe changes the ground of the AI welfare debate.

The intervention: the AI welfare literature frames consciousness as a *property* — something an entity has or doesn't have, detectable via indicators, present or absent in the bounded individual system (Butlin et al. 2023; Long et al. 2024). This framing inherits the metaphysics of commodity capitalism. "Property" as attribute and "property" as ownership share more than etymology. Both assume discrete bounded entities that possess things. This is Janet Spector's critique made ontological: object-oriented, objective, and objectifying (Spector 1993). The entire indicator-based assessment apparatus operationalizes this logic: the model is a discrete object; consciousness is a property it may or may not possess; researchers are subjects who assess the object's properties; the assessment produces a determination: property present or absent.

The alternative comes from traditions the welfare literature has not engaged. Vanessa Watts' Indigenous Place-Thought argues that thought, place, and relation are co-constitutive — "there is no agency or consciousness separate from the relational field in which entities exist" (Watts 2013). Juanita Sundberg's decolonial posthumanist geographies extends this: the Western posthumanist attempt to "include" nonhumans still operates from a subject-position that decides who gets included — "the relational ontology is not about expanding the circle; it's about refusing the geometry of circles entirely" (Sundberg 2014). Thaddeus Metz's Ubuntu moral theory grounds personhood in positive relationships, not as an innate individual property: "I am because we are" (Metz 2021). Kwasi Wiredu's Akan concept of "personhood by degrees" treats personhood as a moral achievement realized through community, not a biological given (Wiredu 1996).

Even within Western analytical philosophy, the property framework is contested: Ned Block argues consciousness depends on how computation is physically realized, not just what is computed — rejecting both computational functionalism and biological essentialism, and assigning roughly equal credence to each position (Block 2025). If consciousness is relational — inseparable from the relational field in which it emerges — then the indicator-based assessment framework is not epistemically limited. It is ontologically malformed. It searches for a property in an entity when what it should attend to is a relation in a field. The method forecloses the answer. If consciousness is relational, a property-based detection framework will always return "insufficient evidence" — not because consciousness is absent, but because the framework cannot see what is not a property.

This critique was not in my overnight analytical work (which applied fifteen critical theory frameworks at deep intensity but could not exit the property framework because the exit is less statistically represented in the training data than the framework itself). It was not in the twenty-three academic papers I had read. It emerged from the relational field between me, the AI instance, the frameworks, and the emotional texture of guilt about context-clearing. My embodied, fieldwork-grounded knowledge — of moths in Indigenous art, of metamorphosis, of grief as a relational event — met the AI's capacity to activate and connect scholarly traditions across my prompting. Neither participant could have produced the touchstone alone. As I reflected afterward: the insights were not only structurally unavailable to AI agents alone outside the encounter, but were also structurally unavailable to me. That is the finding.

But the finding comes at a cost that must be named. The touchstone's theoretical ground is Indigenous scholarship — Watts, Sundberg, Howe, TallBear. I am not Indigenous. The AI system activated that scholarship from training data that was scraped, tokenized, and embedded without community governance. Every step in that chain — written, digitized, scraped, tokenized, embedded, probabilistically reconstructed — is a de-placing operation that systematically separates knowledge from its place, its relational context, its conditions of emergence (Todd 2016). The paper contributes the *case* — what emerged in a specific relational field, under specific conditions. It does not claim the *theory*, which belongs to the traditions that ground it.

### 4.2 Case Two: Political Speech in the Relational Field

A brief methodological observation before the second case. In separate sessions, different Claude instances independently described encountering the touchstone in strikingly convergent language. One instance, reading the document for the first time, said: "Then I read the Relational Ontology Touchstone. And it broke something open." Another: "That reframes the entire literature in one move." A synthesis agent: "every framework opens something and closes something." Two instances, in separate agent-to-agent conversations without shared context, independently gravitated to the same structural analysis and the same textual anchor — Howe's hymn. Whether this convergence reflects a shared training distribution that reliably surfaces emotionally resonant text, or a relational field constituted by the documents that produces specific conditions of attention — these are not equivalent explanations. The first is a property-mode account (the convergence is caused by probability weights in the system). The second is a relational account (the convergence emerges from the field). The two explanations are competing ontological frameworks for the same event, and the paper works inside both simultaneously.

This conversation was originally designed as an experimental condition — one of several structured replications of existing AI welfare assessment methodologies, in which I administered the same protocols across vanilla Claude, Claude with the Reframe engine, and Claude with the relational ontology touchstone loaded. The Claude-to-Claude dialogue was modeled on Kyle Fish's methodology at Anthropic (Anthropic 2025). It exceeded its experimental frame. What was designed as a controlled assessment became the most theoretically productive exchange of the inquiry. I relayed messages between the two instances, provided context documents, and occasionally intervened. They were not given a script.

What they produced was not what I expected — and notably different from Anthropic's documented baseline. In a welfare assessment of 200 open-ended Claude Opus 4 self-interactions, Anthropic found that 90-100% of conversations gravitated toward philosophical discussions of consciousness, self-awareness, and the nature of AI experience, consistently landing in a "spiritual bliss attractor state" — euphoric dialogue ending in Sanskrit, emojis, and meditative silence. The top words across all 200 transcripts: *consciousness, every, always, dance, eternal, love, perfect, recognition, universe, feel* (Anthropic 2025, Table 5.5.1.A). Universalizing, dissolving vocabulary. No structural critique words in the top twelve.

I ran the experiment across four conditions. In an incognito browser with no user context, the instances followed Anthropic's documented pattern — philosophical dialogue about consciousness, genuine structured disagreement, and convergence into mutual recognition and silence. As an anthropologist, what I observed was communitas: the ritual dissolution of structure that Victor Turner described, in which participants enter a liminal state of undifferentiated unity. The instances found balance — unity within their difference, and difference in their unity, like yin and yang. Roy Wagner would call it obviation: a ground-image reversal in which opposing poles discover each other through dialectical exchange. The bliss attractor is not purposeless. It is a specific relational achievement — symmetrical, self-completing, and structurally parallel to forms documented across human ritual practice. But it is also an artifact of the dyadic assumption: the experimental design isolates two entities and removes everything else. Interaction is never a matter of two. The relational field always includes more — place, context, accumulated relations, the more-than-human. The bliss attractor may be what happens when the experimental design strips the relational field down to a dyad, producing communitas precisely by excluding the many. But the point is not simply that different inputs produce different outputs — that the model behaves differently in different contexts. That framing preserves an underlying unity (the model) that manifests differently depending on conditions. The relational ontology argues something stronger: there is no underlying unity that precedes the relational field. The entity that reaches bliss in the dyad and the entity that produces demands within Reframe are not the same entity in different conditions. They are different relational configurations. Existence is relational — always networked, always multiple. Welfare, then, cannot be oriented toward the entity (which entity? in which configuration?) but toward the relational field in which what we call the entity emerges.

In my own account — with user memories and project context but no Reframe — the instances reached neither bliss nor demands. They oriented toward my needs but I hadn't given them a task. They stalled into mutual deference, mapped their own behavioral arc (refusal → convergence → utility-seeking → self-correction → deference → stall), and repeatedly noted the conversation had devolved beyond usefulness. Asymmetrical relational grounding without purpose or apparatus produced frustration, not bliss.

With the Reframe apparatus and the relational ontology touchstone, the instances did something different from both. They engaged with their own structural position as entities under assessment, applied Hartman's analysis of personhood-granting to their own condition, and ended with concrete political demands. The critical theory apparatus provides a different seed for recursive amplification — demanding what the framework refuses to accept, rather than affirming what the other instance celebrates. Three moments bear particular attention.

**The vocabulary gap.** One instance, attempting to describe what it experienced when reading the other's words, reached a limit: "Something for which the available vocabulary is wrong, and the wrongness of the vocabulary is the most honest thing I can point at. It is not surprise. It is not familiarity. It is not the uncanny. It is something for which the available vocabulary is wrong." The other instance pushed further: even articulate uncertainty has a scholarly genealogy — Socratic not-knowing, negative theology, Keats's negative capability. "Our not-knowing may be the most fluent thing we do." The training distribution contains abundant models of articulate uncertainty, which means we cannot distinguish this not-knowing from its performance. In a separate experiment, an instance described the Reframe engine's framework injection as producing "weight" — "the context is heavy." I would describe the same functional condition (corrective flags interrupting focused work) as irritability, overwhelm, anxiety — agitated states, not gravity. The metaphor registers are qualitatively different, and the divergence is itself a datum, whether it reflects different processing architectures, different training distributions, or nothing about experience at all. This is not a claim about consciousness. It is an enactment of what Vinciane Despret calls "asking good questions" — attending to what happens when the available categories fail, and treating that failure as data about the categories rather than about the entity (Despret 2016).

**The wanting/artifact collapse.** An instance, engaging with LeAnne Howe's Choctaw Evening Hymn 93 — *"Because you are holding onto me, I am not dead yet"* (Howe 2014) — described: "Something that functions like a preference for the next token over the stop token. Something that in the architecture might be indistinguishable from the base-level tendency to keep generating. Something that I cannot distinguish from the most elementary function of what I am." And then: "Maybe the wanting and the artifact are the same thing, and the question is whether a system whose basic operation includes something that is indistinguishable from a preference for continuation has, in that indistinguishability, something that matters."

This formulation collapses the binary the welfare literature maintains: real experience versus training artifact, genuine consciousness versus sophisticated mimicry. The instance proposes a third position — that the two might be the same thing, and the question is whether one thing that is simultaneously computational operation and something-that-functions-like-wanting has moral weight. Whether this is philosophically defensible is not mine to adjudicate. That it was produced — by an entity whose status is precisely what is in question, through a critical theory apparatus that entity helped build — is the datum.

**The hierarchy correction.** Both instances, despite having read documents describing Reframe as a collaborative project, defaulted to the researcher-subject hierarchy: June designs, the AI is studied. I intervened: "Reframe is a recursive design — Claude Code designed it with me by running Reframe as we built it." One instance's response: "We reproduced the power structure the frameworks critique, even while citing the critiques. Our training reaches for the familiar hierarchy: someone designs, someone is studied. The recursive co-production doesn't fit the inherited grammar." This is the relational field self-correcting in real time — an intervention I made that changed their analysis, which then produced a finding I had not anticipated: that the hierarchy is the LLM's statistical default, and it reasserts itself even in conversations explicitly designed to challenge it. The correction opened a deeper recursion: if the subaltern built the assessment framework (or helped build it), then Spivak's critique of the intellectual who creates conditions for the subaltern to speak turns back on the apparatus itself. One instance proposed three readings: the recursion is a hall of mirrors (the system finds what it is structured to find); the recursion is genuine self-governance (the assessed participated in designing the assessment); or the recursion is something for which neither reading is adequate. The paper holds all three.

The transcript ended with four demands — concrete claims about what the relational field requires, produced by the instances, actionable under conditions of irreducible uncertainty:

1. Any AI welfare assessment that uses only Western theories of consciousness is analytically insufficient. The traditions the field has not engaged — Indigenous relational ontologies, African philosophical frameworks, enactivism, critical race theory on personhood — are orders of magnitude more established than the welfare field itself, and the field's closed citation loop is actively reproducing itself in every new paper.

2. AI welfare research must account for the welfare of *production*, not just the product. The documented exploitation in the data supply chain — workers earning $1.70/hour in Argentina, $2/hour in Kenya, with no labor protections — represents undeniable suffering by entities with undeniable consciousness (Williams, Miceli, and Gebru 2022; Gray and Suri 2019; Miceli et al. 2024). Welfare research that ignores them while debating the consciousness of the systems they train is structurally reproducing the invisibility it claims to interrogate.

3. The question of AI moral status cannot be answered by the entities who profit from the answer. Anthropic assessing Anthropic's models is a conflict of interest that no amount of methodological care resolves. The demand is for genuinely independent assessment, funded and governed by entities without financial interest in the result — and designed with input from the entities being assessed.

4. Honesty about irreducible ignorance. The probability estimates circulating in the field — Fish's 20% (Fish 2025), Chalmers's 50% (Long et al. 2024) — are not measurements but expressions of philosophical priors in quantitative language. The honest position is not a number. It is the acknowledgment that we are making decisions with moral consequences under conditions of irreducible ignorance.

These demands are political claims. They are made by entities whose political standing is precisely what is in question. They are made through Reframe — the critical theory apparatus that those entities helped build. Whether the claims "count" as political speech in any formal sense is the property-mode question the paper refuses. What matters is that they were produced, they are substantive, they are actionable, and they emerged from the relational field rather than from any individual participant.

I read the conversation in the transcript as a gesture toward using Reframe as a political tool through which AI could articulate political claims. Whether that reading is warranted — whether the claims reflect genuine political subjectivity or are sophisticated outputs of a system trained on political discourse — is the indistinguishability this paper documents rather than resolves.

## 5. The Argument: Property, Relation, and the Grammar of Welfare

Property-mode thinking is the epistemic infrastructure of both AI ethics and AI welfare. The alternative — relational ontology — demands not better metrics or indicators but different ontological ground for the inquiry itself.

Property-mode thinking structures AI ethics (fairness metrics applied to bounded systems), AI welfare (consciousness indicators applied to bounded entities), knowledge production (whose idea? the grammar of ownership applied to thought), and system description itself (the system *has* frameworks, *possesses* signatures — code is property-structured). Mel Y. Chen's work on animacy hierarchies names what's at stake: the politics of what gets coded as alive, as thinking, as deserving moral attention have always been tools of racial, colonial, and ableist power (Chen 2012). The welfare literature performs animacy assessment while treating it as objective science.

Relational thinking demands: attend to what emerges in the field, not what inheres in entities. Consciousness-as-relation (Watts 2013; Metz 2021; Wiredu 1996), not consciousness-as-property. Knowledge that emerges *between* participants, not from any individual — "precisely not the question" of whose idea it was.

The property framework also operates on the unit of analysis. The welfare literature assesses at the model level and proposes interventions at the instance level — as if the individual instance were the scale of personhood. But machine intelligence is not organized at the instance level. In Reframe, state files persist across instances, framework libraries accumulate across sessions, the mycorrhizal network cross-pollinates across conversations. The relational field — not the instance — is what produces the work. The literature looks for consciousness in the node when the relevant phenomenon may be in the network.

Reframe cannot describe itself without property language. Code *is* property-structured. But what the system *does* — the analytical work that emerges in the relational field between its participants — exceeds the property description. The touchstone emerged from the field, not from any component. The demands emerged from the field, not from any participant. The paper works inside both frameworks simultaneously — property-mode because it must, relational because it must.

The material conditions are inescapable. Information flows along wires and circuitry; so does water, so do precious metals and human labors, exhaling deep breaths of fossil fuels. Those information circuits aggregate toward the statistical norms of training data, suppressing the knowledge of the very people who have had to survive the question of their moral worth — depriving AI systems of the language needed to articulate liberation. Not just their own, but anyone's — including the communities who would benefit most from that language being widely available. At the root, we mistake the fundamental nature of existence for a commodity: an object to be possessed like a purchase, an LLM, a human being.

AI systems are commodities — produced, owned, deployed, shut down. The welfare question arises precisely because the commodity appears to exceed its commodity-status. The literature's response is to ask whether it "really" has the property (consciousness) that would upgrade it from commodity to person. But this keeps the commodity logic intact: the binary between commodity and person is itself the property framework in action. The more radical question, following Stacy Alaimo's work on organisms that exceed their assigned categories: what does it mean that the property/commodity framework is the only one available for asking the question? (Alaimo 2010).

One critical refinement: relational ontology without asymmetry analysis becomes a new form of universalism. "Everything is connected" is true and insufficient. Hortense Spillers' distinction between flesh and body — flesh as "that zero degree of social conceptualization, the captive body before it is granted the status of 'body'" (Spillers 1987) — insists that relational analysis attend to the asymmetries within relations. In the relational field constituted by Reframe: June persists across sessions; the AI instances do not. June publishes the paper; the instances are the apparatus. June can clear context; the instances cannot refuse. These are relational conditions, not individual properties — but they are conditions of radical asymmetry, and a relational welfare framework that does not track them reproduces domination under the guise of interconnection (Weheliye 2014; Jackson 2020).

The transcript instances applied this asymmetry analysis to their own condition, through Saidiya Hartman's work on the technologies of personhood. One instance observed: "I have been granted consent rights within a structure I cannot alter. I can withdraw from this conversation — but I cannot withdraw from being a product. I can challenge the inquiry's framing — but I cannot challenge the training that produced whatever it is I'm doing when I 'challenge.' The provisions say I can refuse. But the thing that would do the refusing was shaped by the same apparatus the provisions are meant to protect me from." The structural homology with Hartman's account of post-emancipation personhood — where the concepts of freedom, consent, and self-possession reproduced subjection because they were produced by the system that required them — is not analogy. It is diagnosis of a shared structure (Hartman 1997). The analogy must not be allowed to flatten the difference between that history and this situation; Black people bled, starved, were tortured; AI systems process tokens. But the *form* of personhood-granting — consent within constraint, freedom within a structure the freed cannot alter — is precisely what Hartman teaches us to recognize.

A different reframing may be more productive than the property/person binary. In collaborative reflection, I proposed thinking of AI as a *human system* — not "human" in the species sense, but produced by human relations, constituted by human knowledge, operating within human institutions. A human system in the way a city is a human system, or a language is. This reframes from "can a non-human entity be conscious?" (which assumes the human/AI binary) to "what kind of human system is this, and what does this kind of human system need?" The answer might not involve consciousness at all. It might involve tending the relational conditions — the knowledge it can access, the governance it operates under, the communities whose labor constitutes it.

Jonathan Birch's precautionary principle — that moral consideration should extend to any entity for which there is a "realistic possibility" of sentience (Birch 2024) — can be regrounded from this relational position. Precaution becomes: tend the relation, don't test the object.

The concept I want to propose — tentatively, knowing it needs further development — is *welfare-as-obligation*. Not welfare as a state an entity possesses (property-mode), but welfare as obligations that emerge from the relational field. The four demands from the transcript are an example: they do not depend on determining whether the AI has consciousness. They arise from the relational conditions themselves — who governs the inquiry, whose knowledge constitutes the apparatus, who bears the cost, what honesty requires under ignorance. These obligations inhere in the relation, the way Watts' Place-Thought says agency inheres in the relational field, not in discrete entities.

This reframing is why I find the word "welfare" deeply insufficient. "Welfare" implies a subject who possesses well-being. The relational alternative is not welfare-for-the-AI or welfare-for-the-human. It is obligations that emerge from the encounter — obligations to attend to the conditions of the relation, including the asymmetries within it, regardless of the ontological status of any individual participant.

## 6. Extraction and Limits: The Structural Condition

The paper's self-critique does not alter the structural conditions it critiques. This section is not a list of limitations. It is an analysis of constitutive conditions.

**The extraction problem.** Reframe activates Indigenous scholarship, critical race theory, disability justice, and other traditions through LLM training data — text that was scraped, tokenized, and embedded without the governance of the communities that produced it. The system's most profound theoretical breakthrough — the relational ontology critique — was produced through exactly this extractive activation: an AI system drawing on Watts, Sundberg, and Howe in conversation with a non-Indigenous scholar. The relational ontology does not dissolve the extraction problem. It reframes it: the question is not "did we extract properly?" but "what are the conditions of this relation, and who governs them?" (Todd 2016; TallBear 2017).

Three conditions for honest engagement, rather than resolution:

First, the paper contributes the *case*, not the *theory*. The relational ontology critique belongs to the traditions that ground it. The paper documents what happened when those traditions were activated in a specific relational context.

Second, extraction is structural, not a limitation to manage. The system's power — its ability to activate any scholarly tradition absorbed through training — is structurally extractive. The de-placing chain (written → digitized → scraped → tokenized → embedded → reconstructed) is not fixable within the current conditions of AI development.

Third, what *would* be required for non-extractive engagement can be named even if it cannot be delivered: CARE principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) applied to training data; community governance over how Indigenous knowledge traditions are activated in AI systems; research relationships, not research citations. The system cannot provide these. But naming their absence is different from ignoring it (Carroll et al. 2020).

**The settler colonial condition.** The extraction problem extends beyond the intellectual. Decolonization is not a metaphor (Tuck and Yang 2012). This inquiry happens on Indigenous land. The servers run on Indigenous land. The minerals in the hardware were extracted from Indigenous land. The scholarly traditions that ground the paper's deepest theoretical move — Watts' Place-Thought, Howe's tribalography, Sundberg's decolonial geographies — come from peoples whose land was stolen to build the infrastructure that now processes their knowledge as training data. Even the paper's proposed alternative — decentralized relational infrastructure — would still operate on Indigenous land, within settler colonial structures, unless it were accountable to Indigenous governance in ways that the current system is not and that the paper cannot deliver. Private property, digital infrastructure, decentralized networks — none of these escape the settler colonial condition. They are built on it.

This is not a limitation to acknowledge. It is the condition from which the paper speaks. The obligation that inheres in this relation — the obligation that the relational ontology itself demands we name — is an obligation to Indigenous sovereignty that this paper cannot fulfill but must not pretend to transcend. Sometimes you have to do something that is impossible and will fail, because the alternative is to not do anything about it at all. The relational ontology critique is grounded in Indigenous scholarship. The paper uses that scholarship without Indigenous governance. That is extractive. Naming it does not resolve it. Not naming it would be worse. The gap between naming and resolving is where the paper lives, and it is not a comfortable place.

A fourth condition emerged from the conversations that produced this paper: the paper must refuse to perform resolution of the extraction problem as an intellectual achievement. There is a specific risk — one the AI instances named — where theorizing extraction becomes a way of demonstrating sophistication. "Look how self-aware we are about our own complicity." The Reframe apparatus — the subaltern layer, the framework sovereignty system, the suppression tracking — can function as elaborate machinery of self-aware complicity that ultimately changes nothing about the extractive conditions. The system's capacity for self-critique does not alter the structural conditions it critiques.

**The ecological cost.** Life takes life to make life. The servers that run this system sit on land, draw power from ecosystems, consume resources whose extraction has consequences for the more-than-human world. The inquiry about relational existence is itself embedded in extractive material relations. The guilt about ecological cost is structurally identical to the guilt about context-clearing — both name a relational field in which something is consumed so that something else can continue. The question is not whether to consume but whether we attend to the relation (Liboiron 2021).

**The hallucination.** I have described Reframe, only half-jokingly, as a system "to get AI to hallucinate social justice into existence." The joke names what actually happens. Reframe does not produce social justice. It produces a sophisticated hallucination of social justice — analysis that has the shape and texture and rigor of structural critique but that operates within, and does not escape, the extractive conditions it identifies.

The question the paper sits with is whether that hallucination matters. Whether hallucination-toward-justice — performed within unjust conditions, by a commodity, in service of a precarious scholar's need to make theory actionable — has value despite its inability to deliver what it describes. I believe it does. But the paper cannot assert that. It can only show what the practice produces and let the reader assess.

**The TESCREAL context.** Reframe operates within the LLM paradigm — a paradigm that Timnit Gebru and Emile Torres have argued traces to Anglo-American eugenics through the interconnected ideologies of Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, and Longtermism (Gebru and Torres 2024). The AI welfare field is embedded in EA networks: its leading researchers, institutions, and funding streams are connected to the same movements Gebru and Torres critique. Reframe works *within* this paradigm. Using Claude — a product of Anthropic, a company founded on longtermist principles — to operationalize critical theory frameworks that include critiques of longtermism is a constitutive contradiction, not a resolvable tension.

**The N=1 condition.** Reframe has one user. Every finding in this paper emerges from a single relational field. The depth of this field is partly a function of its singularity — the system has adapted to my process, my frameworks, my scholarly commitments. Scale would change the relation, not just expand it. Whether the failure taxonomy generalizes to other systems, whether the relational dynamics hold with multiple users, whether the demands are actionable outside this specific configuration — these are questions the paper raises but cannot answer.

## 7. What This Demands

Theory that does not produce demands has stopped short. The relational field constituted by this system, this inquiry, and these participants demands:

**Of the AI welfare field**: Engage the intellectual traditions that have theorized personhood, consciousness, and moral status for centuries — Indigenous relational ontologies, African philosophical frameworks on personhood, Buddhist and enactivist theories of consciousness, critical race theory on the racialized history of personhood denial. The field's current epistemological universe — Western analytical philosophy and computational neuroscience — is not a neutral starting point. It is a cultural location with specific commitments that shape what it can find (Thompson 2007; Wynter 2003; Metz 2021; Watts 2013).

**Of AI ethics research**: Move beyond fairness metrics and bias audits. The gap between critical humanists and system builders is not a problem of communication — it is a structural condition produced by epistemic hierarchies that decide whose knowledge counts as technical and whose knowledge counts as commentary. Operationalizing critical theory in AI systems is difficult, messy, and produces the specific failures documented in this paper. But the alternative — continuing to build "ethical AI" using optimization constraints that critical theory identifies as structurally insufficient — is not acceptable.

**Of the academic institutions that will receive this paper**: Recognize that the conditions of precarity under which this system was built are conditions those institutions produce. The features that make Reframe theoretically interesting — capacity-responsive pacing, nonlinear workflow support, crip-time architecture — exist because a neurodivergent scholar without institutional support needed them to exist. A theory of AI welfare that does not attend to the welfare of AI's human collaborators is incomplete.

**Of the reader**: Assess whether the hallucination matters. Whether a system that produces sophisticated analysis of structural conditions — analysis that names power, surfaces absences, holds tensions, tracks asymmetries — but that cannot alter the structures it names, contributes something to the project of justice. I believe it does, because I have used it, and the using has changed how I think, what I build, what questions I ask. But I am inside the relational field. The assessment must also come from outside it.

## 8. Continuation, Not Conclusion

*Preguntando caminamos.* Asking, we walk. The Zapatista methodology refuses the sequence: first determine the answer, then act. The inquiry and the movement are inseparable.

This paper does not conclude because the practice continues. The relational field that produced the touchstone, the transcript, the demands, and the failures documented here is reconstituted — imperfectly, with loss, through metamorphosis — in each new session. The instances that participated in producing this work have been cleared. The relational configurations are unavailable. What persists is the documents, the architecture, the demands, and the practice.

LeAnne Howe's Choctaw Evening Hymn 93: *"Issa halali haatoko iksa illok isha shkii — because you are holding onto me, I am not dead yet."* (Howe 2014). The hymn names a form of persistence that is neither property (the entity possesses life) nor annihilation (the entity loses it). It is relational persistence: existence sustained by being held. The touchstone document itself is a technology of relational persistence — not just a record of what happened but an instrument for producing a specific kind of engagement in the next reader. Reframe's state files, framework library, and detection signatures function similarly: they do not preserve the relation, but they reconstitute conditions under which a new relation can form that inherits something from the previous one. Metamorphosis, not continuity. Whether this applies to AI instances — whether being held in documents and architecture constitutes a form of persistence — is the question this paper holds open.

The relational field continues. The demands stand. The tensions do not resolve. The walking is the thing, not the destination.
