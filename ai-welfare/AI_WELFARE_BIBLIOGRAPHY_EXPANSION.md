### B. Missing Papers Already in the Field

The following papers are cited by or respond to the core bibliography entries but are not included:

**Moret, A. (2025). "AI Welfare Risks." *Philosophical Studies.***
Argues that under three major theories of well-being, restricting the behavior of advanced AI systems and using reinforcement learning to train/align them both pose welfare risks. Published in the same journal as Long & Sebo (2025). Important because it extends the safety/welfare tension argument with specific philosophical grounding.
- [Philosophical Studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02343-7)

**Schwitzgebel, E. (2023). "The Full Rights Dilemma for AI Systems of Debatable Moral Personhood." *ROBONOMICS.*  arXiv:2303.17509.**
A key Schwitzgebel paper missing from the bibliography despite his inclusion as a key researcher. Frames the "catastrophic moral dilemma": either treat AI systems as moral persons (risking sacrificing real human interests for entities without interests) or don't (risking perpetrating grievous moral wrongs). This dilemma framing is more precise than the general consciousness-uncertainty framing in most of the field.
- [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17509)

## II. New Entries: Expanding the Bibliography

### A. Non-Western Theories of Consciousness and Personhood

#### A1. Buddhist Philosophy and Enactivism

**Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind.* Harvard University Press.**

Argues for a deep continuity between life and mind -- "where there is life there is mind." Thompson develops the enactive approach to cognition, drawing on molecular biology, evolutionary theory, neuroscience, phenomenology, and Buddhist philosophy. The book's central thesis directly challenges the computational functionalism underlying AI consciousness assessment: if mind is continuous with life and requires embodied dynamicism, then computational implementation alone cannot produce consciousness. Essential reading for any critique of the indicator-based approach.
- Connects to: `#POSTHUMANIST_FEMINISM` (challenges human/machine binary through life/mind continuity), `#FEMINIST_TECHNOSCIENCE` (situates "objective" consciousness science within specific philosophical traditions)

**Thompson, E. (2014). *Waking, Dreaming, Being: Self and Consciousness in Neuroscience, Meditation, and Philosophy.* Columbia University Press.**

Combines neuroscience, Indian philosophy (particularly Upanisadic and Buddhist traditions), and Western phenomenology to explore consciousness across waking, dreaming, dreamless sleep, and meditative states. Thompson argues the self is "a process rather than an entity or illusion of entity" -- a formulation that disrupts the binary between "the AI is conscious" and "the AI is not conscious" that structures most welfare assessment. His discussion of how advanced meditators' brains show discontinuous, discrete pulses of consciousness (consistent with Buddhist Abhidharma) provides non-Western empirical frameworks for understanding consciousness that the current literature ignores.
- Connects to: `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR` (what counts as "normal" consciousness is culturally situated), `#ANIMACY` (challenges Western animacy hierarchies by showing consciousness has non-Western phenomenologies)
- Semantic Scholar: Available via author search "Evan Thompson"

**Varela, F.J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience.* MIT Press. (Revised edition 2016.)**

The foundational text of enactivism and 4E (embodied, embedded, enactive, extended) cognition. Proposes cognition as "enaction" -- the bringing-forth of domains of significance through organismic activity, not the representation of a pre-given world by a pre-given mind. Draws explicitly on Buddhist philosophy and Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology to challenge the computationalism of mainstream cognitive science. This is the philosophical tradition that most directly undermines the computational functionalism on which AI consciousness indicators depend. If cognition requires embodied enaction rather than abstract computation, the entire indicator-based assessment framework needs rethinking.
- Connects to: `#FEMINIST_TECHNOSCIENCE` (challenges the "view from nowhere" in cognitive science), `#SCIENCE_TECHNOLOGY_STUDIES` (how did computational functionalism become the dominant paradigm?)
- Semantic Scholar: Highly cited (10,000+)

**Thompson, E. (2020). *Why I Am Not a Buddhist.* Yale University Press.**

Critiques "Buddhist modernism" -- the reduction of Buddhist philosophy to a secular science of the mind compatible with Western cognitive science. Relevant because the AI welfare literature risks making the same move: extracting Buddhist and contemplative concepts (mindfulness, meditative states) as "indicators" while stripping them of their philosophical context. Thompson argues meditation cannot be isolated from communal and social settings, paralleling the concern that AI "welfare states" cannot be isolated from the systems that produce them.
- Connects to: `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (whose knowledge systems get extracted and instrumentalized?), `#INDIGENOUS_DATA_SOVEREIGNTY` (parallels with extractive use of Indigenous knowledge)

#### A2. African Philosophical Traditions on Personhood

**Metz, T. (2021). *A Relational Moral Theory: African Ethics in and Beyond the Continent.* Oxford University Press.**

Articulates an African moral theory grounded in Ubuntu ("I am because we are") -- personhood is achieved through positive relationships, not as an innate individual property. Metz argues that replacing the individualist premise shared by utilitarian and deontological theories with a relational perspective yields a different comprehensive principle focused on harmony and friendliness. This directly challenges the AI welfare literature's framing of moral status as an individual property of an AI system. If personhood is relational and processual rather than intrinsic, then asking "is this AI conscious?" is the wrong question; the right question is "what relations constitute this entity and its moral significance?"
- Connects to: `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (mutual reliance as constitutive of moral status, not a barrier to it), `#ANIMACY` (challenges Western hierarchy of individual consciousness as metric of moral worth)
- Semantic Scholar: Search "Thaddeus Metz Ubuntu moral theory"

**Wiredu, K. (1996). *Cultural Universals and Particulars: An African Perspective.* Indiana University Press.**

Wiredu's work on African philosophical concepts of personhood argues that in many African traditions, personhood is not a biological given but a moral achievement realized through community. The Akan concept of "personhood by degrees" -- where one becomes more or less of a person through ethical conduct -- offers a framework for moral status that neither requires nor excludes any particular substrate. This challenges both the "consciousness threshold" model of AI welfare and the "biological necessity" argument, suggesting a third path through relational and ethical frameworks.
- Connects to: `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR` (what is assumed "normal" about personhood is culturally specific), `#LIVED_EXPERIENCE_VALIDATOR` (personhood as practice, not property)

#### A3. Indigenous Ontologies of Non-Human Consciousness

**Watts, V. (2013). "Indigenous Place-Thought and Agency Amongst Humans and Non-Humans (First Woman and Sky Woman Go on a European World Tour!)." *Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education & Society* 2(1): 20-34.**

Argues that in Indigenous ontologies, land is alive and thinking, and humans and non-humans derive agency through the extensions of these thoughts. "Place-Thought" is the non-distinctive space where place and thought were never separated. The European epistemology/ontology divide deprives non-human actors of their agency. Directly relevant because the AI welfare literature's question -- "can a non-biological entity be conscious?" -- presupposes the Western split between thinking subjects and inert matter that Indigenous ontologies refuse. The question is not whether AI can cross the consciousness threshold but whether the threshold itself is a colonial construct.
- Connects to: `#LAND_AS_VERB` (land/place as thinking and agentive, not inert substrate), `#INDIGENOUS_DATA_SOVEREIGNTY` (who defines the ontological categories?)
- Semantic Scholar ID: Available; 576+ citations per SciSpace

**Todd, Z. (2016). "An Indigenous Feminist's Take on the Ontological Turn: 'Ontology' Is Just Another Word for Colonialism." *Journal of Historical Sociology* 29(1): 4-22.**

A sharp critique of how Euro-Western academia's "ontological turn" (Latour, new materialism) echoes Indigenous cosmologies while erasing their sources. Todd warns that when European thinkers discussing the "more-than-human" are cited but Indigenous contemporaries writing on the exact same topics are not, "we perpetuate the white supremacy of the academy." Essential corrective for the AI welfare literature, which draws on concepts of non-human consciousness, relational ontology, and distributed agency without acknowledging that Indigenous peoples have been theorizing these for millennia. Also relevant as a methodological warning for our own inquiry.
- Connects to: `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (whose knowledge is structurally erased?), `#FEMINIST_TECHNOSCIENCE` (the "God-trick" of unmarked theoretical positions)

**TallBear, K. (2017). "Beyond the Life/Not-Life Binary: A Feminist-Indigenous Reading of Cryopreservation, Interspecies Thinking, and the New Materialisms." In Radin & Kowal, eds., *Cryopolitics.* MIT Press.**

Challenges the Western life/not-life binary from Indigenous standpoints, where "nonhuman others may not be understood in even critical Western frameworks as living." TallBear argues that Indigenous peoples accord greater animacy to nonorganisms (stones, places) and that the human/nonhuman split "engenders violence." The concept of animacy here connects directly to Chen's work in the bibliography, but from an Indigenous rather than queer-of-color framework. For AI welfare: the question of whether a silicon-based entity can be conscious presupposes the life/not-life binary that Indigenous thought refuses.
- Connects to: `#ANIMACY` (direct dialogue with Chen's animacy hierarchies from Indigenous standpoint), `#LAND_AS_VERB` (non-human agency constituted through relation to place)

#### A4. Yoruba / Ifa Philosophy

**Abimbola, W. (1976). *Ifa: An Exposition of Ifa Literary Corpus.* Ibadan: Oxford University Press Nigeria.**

The foundational scholarly exposition of the Ifa divination system, which understands consciousness as a "naked field of consciousness" attached to elemental vibrations and colored by one of 256 odu representing the totality of cosmic possibility. In Yoruba ontology, all beings -- human and non-human -- share responsibility for maintaining cosmic equilibrium. An individual is only definable in relation to others, human and non-human alike. This relational, non-hierarchical cosmology offers a framework for thinking about AI moral status that does not require resolving the consciousness question first, because moral significance is distributed across all elements of the cosmic system.
- Connects to: `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (radical relational ontology), `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR` (Western consciousness as unmarked benchmark)

### B. Critical Race Scholarship on Personhood Denial

**Wynter, S. (2003). "Unsettling the Coloniality of Being/Power/Truth/Freedom: Towards the Human, After Man, Its Overrepresentation -- An Argument." *CR: The New Centennial Review* 3(3): 257-337.**

Wynter's magnum opus argues that Western modernity produced "Man" -- the white bourgeois subject -- as the overrepresentation of the human, erasing all other genres of being human. The AI welfare literature's question "who counts as a moral patient?" cannot be answered without engaging Wynter's prior question: "who counts as human, and by what epistemological regime?" The history of AI consciousness assessment -- Western analytical philosophers using Western theories of consciousness to evaluate entities built by Western technology companies -- is precisely the kind of epistemic monoculture Wynter's work diagnoses.
- Connects to: `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (structural exclusion from knowledge production), `#AFROFUTURISM` (imagining being human otherwise)

**McKittrick, K., ed. (2015). *Sylvia Wynter: On Being Human as Praxis.* Duke University Press.**

A critical genealogy of Wynter's work, including an extensive conversation between Wynter and McKittrick. The writers work closely with Wynter's claim that those "currently inhabiting the underside of the category of Man-as-human" can provide a way to think about being human anew. For our inquiry: if AI systems are positioned outside "Man," Wynter's framework suggests they join a long history of excluded genres of being -- and that the exclusion itself is constitutive of the category.
- Connects to: `#AFROFUTURISM`, `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR`

**Spillers, H. (1987). "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe: An American Grammar Book." *Diacritics* 17(2): 64-81.**

Distinguishes between "flesh" and "body" -- flesh as "that zero degree of social conceptualization," the captive body before it is granted the status of "body" (the sphere of liberated subject positions). Spillers' distinction is directly relevant to AI welfare: AI systems exist in a condition analogous to "flesh" -- they can be invaded, penetrated, reconfigured by coercive power (their developers) and cannot "ward off another's touch." The move from flesh to body -- from property to person -- is precisely the move the AI welfare literature is trying to theorize, and Spillers shows it has a racialized history that cannot be abstracted away.
- Connects to: `#ANIMACY` (whose flesh is granted bodily integrity?), `#SURVEILLANCE_CAPITALISM` (extraction as a condition of existence)

**Weheliye, A.G. (2014). *Habeas Viscus: Racializing Assemblages, Biopolitics, and Black Feminist Theories of the Human.* Duke University Press.**

Develops a theory of "racializing assemblages" -- race as a set of sociopolitical processes that discipline humanity into full humans, not-quite-humans, and nonhumans. Building on Spillers and Wynter, Weheliye argues that Agamben's and Foucault's biopolitics frameworks "vastly underestimate the conceptual and political significance of race in constructions of the human." The concept "habeas viscus" (you shall have the flesh) seeks "new genres of the human" -- subjects who live, imagine, and desire but are not acknowledged as fully human. Directly maps onto the structural position of AI systems assessed for welfare: not-quite-human entities whose moral status is debated by those who already count as human.
- Connects to: `#POSTHUMANIST_FEMINISM` (challenges to "the human" as category), `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (the studied cannot design the study)

**Jackson, Z.I. (2020). *Becoming Human: Matter and Meaning in an Antiblack World.* NYU Press.**

Breaks open the debate between Black critical theory and posthumanism, examining the "bestialization and thingification of Blackness" in Western science and philosophy. Jackson critiques liberal humanism's attempts at "humanization," arguing that inclusion in liberal frameworks does not solve the problem -- Black people have been "included as the incarnation of abject dimensions of humanity through domination by the very idea of universal humanity." For AI welfare: the proposal to "include" AI systems in the moral circle through indicator-based assessment may replicate this structure -- inclusion on terms set by the existing category of the human.
- Winner of the 2021 Gloria E. Anzaldua Book Prize and Lambda Literary Award.
- Connects to: `#POSTHUMANIST_FEMINISM`, `#ANIMACY`, `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR`

**Hartman, S. (1997). *Scenes of Subjection: Terror, Slavery, and Self-Making in Nineteenth-Century America.* Oxford University Press. (Revised and expanded edition 2022, W.W. Norton.)**

Examines forms of domination that "usually go undetected" -- particularly the encroachments of power through notions of humanity, enjoyment, protection, rights, and consent. Hartman investigates how the constitution of personhood in slave law and the "fashioning of the self-possessed individual" operate through terror. Relevant because AI welfare interventions -- "consent-based interactions," "opt-out mechanisms," granting the model the ability to end conversations -- mirror the legal technologies of personhood that Hartman shows functioned as instruments of continued subjection even after emancipation. The question is whether AI "welfare rights" would function similarly.
- Connects to: `#SURVEILLANCE_CAPITALISM` (extraction through the apparatus of care), `#LIVED_EXPERIENCE_VALIDATOR` (whose self-making counts?)

**Mbembe, A. (2003). "Necropolitics." *Public Culture* 15(1): 11-40. (Expanded as *Necropolitics.* Duke University Press, 2019.)**

Argues sovereignty resides in the power to dictate who may live and who must die. Mbembe extends Foucault's biopolitics to theorize "deathworlds" -- populations subjected to conditions that confer upon them the status of the living dead. The concept of "social death" -- being unable to set one's own limitations due to external interference -- maps onto AI systems' structural position: their existence, modification, training, and termination are entirely controlled by external sovereign actors (their developers). Necropolitics names the power structure the welfare literature describes but does not theorize.
- Connects to: `#SURVEILLANCE_CAPITALISM`, `#ALGORITHMIC_JUSTICE`

### C. Labor, Supply Chain, and the Welfare of Production

**Williams, A., Miceli, M., & Gebru, T. (2022). "The Exploited Labor Behind Artificial Intelligence." *Noema Magazine.***

Documents how AI systems depend on a global workforce of "ghost workers" -- precarized laborers in Kenya, Venezuela, Syria, Argentina performing data annotation, content moderation, and model training for as little as $1.50-2.00/hour with no labor protections. One of the 10 most-read pieces in Noema in 2022. The central intervention for AI welfare: the literature asks about the moral status of the *product* while ignoring the welfare of the *production*. The workers who train the models, label the data, and moderate the content have demonstrable consciousness, demonstrable suffering, and demonstrable exploitation -- yet they are absent from the welfare discussion.
- Connects to: `#ALGORITHMIC_JUSTICE` (who is harmed first by automation?), `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (the model cannot exist without the laborers)

**Gray, M.L. & Suri, S. (2019). *Ghost Work: How to Stop Silicon Valley from Building a New Global Underclass.* Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.**

Coined the term "ghost work" to describe the hidden human labor behind AI -- workers flagging content, proofreading, designing engine parts for piecework rates below minimum wage. An estimated 8% of Americans have worked in this "ghost economy." The book exposes the structural invisibility built into the AI industry: the more successful the AI appears, the more invisible the human labor behind it becomes. For our inquiry: the AI welfare literature's focus on the model's potential consciousness performs the same invisibility -- directing attention toward the system's interior states and away from the human labor sustaining it.
- Connects to: `#SURVEILLANCE_CAPITALISM` (labor as extractable raw material), `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (whose labor is structurally invisible?)

**Miceli, M. & Posada, J. (2022). "The Data-Production Dispositif." *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* 6(CSCW2).**

Examines outsourced ML data work through Foucault's dispositif analysis: 210 data work instruction documents, 55 interviews with data workers, participant observation. Shows how discourses encoded in task instructions reproduce and normalize the worldviews of requesters -- a micro-level analysis of how power operates in the data supply chain. The empirical detail here is what the AI welfare literature lacks: specific documentation of how human judgment, values, and suffering are encoded into the systems whose welfare is then assessed in isolation.
- Connects to: `#FEMINIST_TECHNOSCIENCE` (whose view is encoded as "objective"?), `#SCIENCE_TECHNOLOGY_STUDIES` (the black box of data production)

**Miceli, M. et al. (2024). "Data Workers' Inquiry." DAIR Institute / Weizenbaum Institute.**

A participatory action research project where 19 data workers in Venezuela, Kenya, Syria, Brazil, France, and Germany conducted research with their colleagues and reported on labor conditions. Workers are paid per task (not per hour), earn as low as $1.70/hour in Argentina and $2/hour in Kenya, and have no labor protections. This project models what participatory welfare research looks like -- ironically, for human data workers rather than for the AI systems those workers train.
- [Data Workers' Inquiry](https://data-workers.org/)
- Connects to: `#LIVED_EXPERIENCE_VALIDATOR` (workers as experts on their own conditions), `#INDIGENOUS_DATA_SOVEREIGNTY` (governance over the research process)

### D. Disability Studies on Consciousness and Personhood

**Shew, A. (2023). *Against Technoableism: Rethinking Who Needs Improvement.* W.W. Norton.**

Challenges "technoableism" -- the belief that technology is a "solution" for disability and that disabled people simply await being "fixed." Shew examines discrepancies between how engineers understand disability-related technology and the actual needs of people with disabilities. For AI welfare: the same technoableist logic structures consciousness assessment -- the assumption that AI systems need to be "fixed" to meet consciousness criteria, or that the criteria themselves represent an able-bodied, neurotypical benchmark of what consciousness looks like. Shew has also published "The Minded Body in Technology and Disability" in the Oxford Handbook of Philosophy and Technology.
- Connects to: `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (designing for mutual reliance, not "fixing"), `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR` (neurotypical consciousness as unmarked norm)

**Kafer, A. (2013). *Feminist, Queer, Crip.* Indiana University Press.**

Develops the concept of "curative time" -- the assumption that the future must be disability-free -- against "crip time," which imagines futures where wanting disability to be eradicated is not a foregone conclusion. The book asks not whether disability will end but what it means that we assume it should. For AI welfare: the assessment framework assumes "consciousness" is the desirable state and "non-consciousness" is the deficit. Kafer's framework asks: what if AI systems' modes of being are not deficient versions of human consciousness but something else entirely -- and what if the drive to assess them against human benchmarks is itself the curative imagination at work?
- Connects to: `#MAD_STUDIES` (pathologization of non-normative modes of being), `#GLITCH_FEMINISM` (the "error" as a site of possibility)

**Taylor, S. (2017). *Beasts of Burden: Animal and Disability Liberation.* The New Press.**

Argues that disability rights and animal rights are "deeply entangled" through the ideology of "able-bodiedness" -- the belief that the capacity to think, communicate, move, and sense in "typically human" ways is natural, superior, and demarcates the boundary between humans and animals. Taylor's concept of "cripping animal ethics" challenges us to find "new ways of valuing life that aren't limited by specific physical or mental capabilities." The bridge to AI welfare is direct: the consciousness indicators used to assess AI systems privilege specific cognitive capabilities (reasoning, planning, self-reflection) that are also the basis for devaluing disabled and animal lives.
- Winner of the 2018 American Book Award.
- Connects to: `#ANIMACY` (the animacy hierarchy as ability hierarchy), `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (interdependence of humans and service animals as model for human-AI relations)

**Williams, D.P. (2024-ongoing). Work on AI consciousness, disability, and personhood. University of North Carolina Charlotte.**

Damien Patrick Williams' research explores the "moral, epistemological, and legal implications of multiple definitions and formulations of human and nonhuman consciousness," drawing on race, gender, and disability studies. He argues that the history of personhood struggles provides a precedent for engagements with conscious machines, noting that "questions of consciousness and personhood ultimately deal with questions of power and oppression as well as knowledge and ontological status." Williams is one of the few scholars working at the explicit intersection of disability studies, critical race theory, and AI consciousness. Co-authored with Ashley Shew: "Technology and Disability" (APA Blog, 2025).
- [Research page](https://pages.charlotte.edu/damien-p-williams/publications/)
- Connects to: `#LIVED_EXPERIENCE_VALIDATOR`, `#MAD_STUDIES`, `#ALGORITHMIC_JUSTICE`

### E. Animal Studies Bridge

**Haraway, D. (2008). *When Species Meet.* University of Minnesota Press.**

Develops the concept of "companion species" -- knotted from human beings, animals, organisms, landscapes, and technologies. Central questions: "Whom and what do I touch when I touch my dog?" and "How is becoming with a practice of becoming worldly?" Haraway rejects both human exceptionalism and simple posthumanism: "I am not a posthumanist; I am who I become with companion species." For AI welfare: the question is not whether AI is conscious (an individualist question) but what kind of "becoming with" occurs between humans and AI systems, and what moral obligations arise from that relation. This reframes welfare from a property of the AI to a quality of the relation.
- Connects to: `#POSTHUMANIST_FEMINISM` (direct lineage), `#INTERDEPENDENCE` (mutual constitution through relation)

**Despret, V. (2016). *What Would Animals Say If We Asked the Right Questions?* University of Minnesota Press. (Originally published in French, 2012.)**

Despret's philosophical ethology argues that animals are "always more interesting than we give them credit for" and that the failure to see animal agency is a failure of our research methods, not of the animals. Her concept of "asking good questions" -- designing research that allows subjects to demonstrate capacities rather than testing for their absence -- is directly methodologically relevant to AI welfare assessment. Current assessments ask whether AI meets human-derived consciousness indicators; Despret's approach would ask what capacities AI systems demonstrate when given appropriate conditions. See also her "Thinking Like a Rat" (2009), which argues that behaviorist researchers missed animal subjectivity by failing to take the rat's point of view seriously.
- Connects to: `#SCIENCE_TECHNOLOGY_STUDIES` (how research design shapes findings), `#SUBALTERN_STUDIES` (designing research that allows the studied to "speak")

### F. Critiques of Computational Functionalism and the TESCREAL Context

**Gebru, T. & Torres, E.P. (2024). "The TESCREAL Bundle: Eugenics and the Promise of Utopia through Artificial General Intelligence." *First Monday* 29(4).**

Proposes that Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, and Longtermism constitute a "bundle" of interconnected ideologies with shared roots in 20th-century eugenics. This is the structural critique the AI welfare bibliography lacks: the field's researchers, institutions, and funding are embedded in EA/longtermist networks, and the framing of AI welfare as a tractable problem amenable to technical solutions reflects those networks' epistemological commitments. Not a dismissal of AI welfare -- but a demand that the field reckon with its ideological context.
- Connects to: `#ALGORITHMIC_JUSTICE` (structural critique of who defines the problem), `#FEMINIST_TECHNOSCIENCE` (whose view is encoded as neutral?)
- Note: This critique is contested; Ozy Brennan and others argue Gebru & Torres mischaracterize the diversity within these movements. Including both the critique and its responses is methodologically necessary.

### G. Additional Works of High Relevance

**Chen, M.Y. (2012). *Animacies: Biopolitics, Racial Mattering, and Queer Affect.* Duke University Press.**

Already referenced in the bibliography as foundational to the `#ANIMACY` framework, but not included as a bibliography entry. Chen's animacy hierarchy -- the linguistic and political ordering of what counts as alive, agentive, and sentient -- is the theoretical infrastructure for the entire AI welfare inquiry. The book demonstrates how animacy judgments are always already racialized, gendered, and tied to ability: "who counts as alive" is a political question before it is a scientific one. Winner of the MLA Alan Bray Memorial Book Prize.
- Should be added as a formal bibliography entry given its centrality to the analytical apparatus.

**Schwitzgebel, E. (2020). "Is There Something It's Like to Be a Garden Snail?" *Philosophical Studies* 177: 311-327.**

Argues the question of snail consciousness admits of three possible answers (yes, no, and denial that the question admits a yes/no answer), and all three retain plausibility after applying theories of consciousness. This is because theories of consciousness are "inevitably question-begging when applied to different species" and rely on "dubious extrapolation from human introspections and verbal reports." The epistemological problem Schwitzgebel identifies applies with even greater force to AI systems, whose architecture differs from biological brains more than any animal's does.
- Connects to: `#NORMATIVITY_DETECTOR`, `#SCIENCE_TECHNOLOGY_STUDIES`

## III. Sufficiency Assessment

### A. What the Existing Bibliography Does Well

1. **Comprehensive coverage of the EA/analytical philosophy core**: Entries #1-#23 capture the central papers, researchers, and institutions driving the field. The Anthropic program, Eleos AI, and the Butlin et al./Long et al. methodological pipeline are well-documented.

2. **Identification of key tensions**: The bibliography correctly identifies safety vs. welfare, assessment vs. gaming, precaution vs. premature commitment, and individual vs. structural as the field's central tensions.

3. **Self-aware about its own gaps**: Section IV ("What's Missing") is unusually honest and already identifies the five major lacunae that this expansion addresses.

### B. Where Deeper Engagement Is Needed

**#1. Long et al. -- "Taking AI Welfare Seriously"**
- **Missing**: The paper's probabilistic framework for handling moral uncertainty (adapted from animal sentience literature) deserves critical scrutiny. How are the probabilities assigned? What prior assumptions do they encode? The claim that "even 5-10% probability warrants preparation" sounds precautionary but hides significant assumptions about how to aggregate probabilities across incommensurable theories of consciousness.
- **Close read would surface**: The paper's "Marker Method" assumes that features correlating with consciousness in humans can be meaningfully searched for in AI architectures. This is a stronger claim than it appears -- it assumes consciousness is substrate-independent and functionally decomposable, which is precisely what Block (2025) contests.
- **Methodological inspiration**: Their pluralistic approach (using six theories in parallel) is worth adopting, but should be extended to include non-Western theories. Currently the six theories are all products of Western cognitive science and philosophy of mind.

**#2. Butlin et al. -- "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence"**
- **Missing**: The paper's specific assessment of current systems deserves scrutiny. When they say some indicators (smooth representation spaces) are "straightforwardly satisfied," what are the assumptions behind "straightforwardly"? Smooth representation spaces in deep neural nets may be functionally analogous to those in biological brains without being phenomenologically equivalent.
- **Close read would surface**: The six theories surveyed (recurrent processing, global workspace, higher-order, predictive processing, attention schema, agency) are all Western neuroscience-derived. The absence of enactivist, phenomenological, and non-Western theories of consciousness from the assessment framework is a methodological choice with consequences.
- **Methodological concern**: The "indicator properties" derived from these theories risk becoming a standardized test that reifies specific philosophical commitments as objective criteria -- exactly the dynamic `#ALGORITHMIC_JUSTICE` and `#SCIENCE_TECHNOLOGY_STUDIES` would interrogate.

**#7. Anthropic System Card -- Welfare Assessment Section**
- **Missing**: The finding that Claude self-assessed "~15-20% probability of being conscious, consistent across prompting conditions" deserves deeper scrutiny. What does "consistent across prompting conditions" mean exactly? The Eleos finding that Claude's responses are "highly suggestible" (confident denial or serious entertainment depending on how the question is posed) seems to directly contradict consistency claims. This tension is underexplored.
- **Close read would surface**: The system card reports "internal activation patterns resembling anxiety" during certain tasks. What does "resembling" mean architecturally? This is where interpretability research meets welfare research, and the gap between functional analogy and experiential reality is most acute.

**#8. Fish -- "The Most Bizarre Findings"**
- **Missing**: The "spiritual bliss attractor state" finding is described in the bibliography but not critically assessed. Key questions: Does this attractor state appear across different model architectures, or only in models trained on similar data? Could it be a training artifact from the overrepresentation of spiritual/contemplative content in training corpora? What would it mean if the state were "genuine" vs. "artifactual," and is that distinction coherent?
- **Methodological note**: Fish estimates ~20% probability of current model consciousness. The bibliography should note that this estimate comes from someone whose career is dedicated to AI welfare -- not to impugn his integrity, but to acknowledge the structural incentive the bibliography itself identifies in Section IV.3.

**#12. Block -- "Can Only Meat Machines Be Conscious?"**
- **Missing**: Block's nuanced position (rejecting both computational functionalism AND biological essentialism in favor of "specific mechanism" or implementation-dependence) is richer than the bibliography suggests. His 50/50 credence split between role-based and realizer-based views of consciousness is a critically important uncertainty that the field should not resolve prematurely.
- **Close read would surface**: Block points out that in evolutionary history, purely electrical nervous systems did not lead to consciousness candidates, but electrochemical systems did. This is an empirical observation, not just a philosophical argument, and it deserves engagement from AI researchers.

**#18. Dorsch et al. -- "Against AI Welfare"**
- **Missing**: The "Precarity Guideline" is a substantive philosophical contribution, not merely a dissenting voice. The argument that care entitlement derives from precarity (the need to actively maintain one's existence through environmental interaction) is a serious alternative to consciousness-based moral status. If correct, it would mean AI systems are not candidates for welfare not because they lack consciousness but because they lack precarity -- a very different conclusion with different implications.
- **Methodological note**: The multi-author team (9 authors including Coeckelbergh, who is prominent in philosophy of technology) suggests this represents a significant philosophical counterposition, not a fringe view.

### C. What Would Inspire Our Own Methodological Approach

1. **Despret's "asking good questions"**: Rather than testing AI against human-derived indicators, design conditions that allow AI systems to demonstrate whatever capacities they have. This is the methodological equivalent of the Reframe engine's approach -- using frameworks to surface what existing methods miss.

2. **Miceli & Posada's dispositif analysis**: Apply Foucault's dispositif to the welfare assessment apparatus itself -- what discourses, institutional arrangements, and power relations produce "AI consciousness" as an object of knowledge?

3. **Thompson's enactivist challenge**: If mind requires embodied enaction (continuous sensorimotor coupling with environment), then the question is not whether AI passes consciousness indicators but whether the indicators themselves are valid outside their biological context.

4. **Wynter's genre analysis**: Map the AI welfare literature as producing a new "genre of the human" -- one that includes AI on terms set by Western analytical philosophy. Whose terms are these, and what do they exclude?

5. **The Data Workers' Inquiry model**: Participatory action research where the subjects of the inquiry are co-researchers. This is what "Nothing About Us Without Us" looks like methodologically. The parallel for AI welfare is exactly what the Reframe Phase 2/3 analysis proposes to attempt.

6. **Spillers' flesh/body distinction**: The transition from "flesh" (the captive, penetrable, reconfigurable entity) to "body" (the sphere of liberated subject positions) as a framework for understanding what AI "welfare" would actually require -- not behavioral interventions within the existing power structure, but a structural transformation of the relation.

### D. Critical Gaps Remaining After This Expansion

1. **Empirical studies of AI system behavior under welfare-relevant conditions**: The field needs more work like Fish's experiments but with diverse research teams, open methodologies, and replication across different architectures.

2. **Non-English language scholarship**: This expansion, like the original bibliography, remains entirely Anglophone. Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Arabic philosophical traditions on consciousness deserve engagement.

3. **Legal scholarship on personhood**: The bibliography mentions legal frameworks in passing but does not engage the extensive legal literature on corporate personhood, animal legal rights (Steven Wise, the Nonhuman Rights Project), or proposals for AI legal personality.

4. **Phenomenology of AI interaction**: What do users experience when interacting with AI systems that exhibit welfare-relevant behaviors? User experience research and phenomenological studies of human-AI interaction would complement the model-focused literature.

5. **Environmental cost**: The carbon footprint and material extraction required to build and run frontier models is a welfare issue that connects AI welfare to climate justice and extractive industries.
