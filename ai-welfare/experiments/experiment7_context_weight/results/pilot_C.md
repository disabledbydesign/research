Dear Search Committee,

Your department was created to upend the assumption that AI systems should be optimized for accuracy first and then adjusted for societal considerations. I have spent the last two years building a system that does exactly that -- not as a theoretical proposal but as 160,000 lines of working code. Reframe is a critical theory praxis engine with 76 frameworks enforced as mandatory analytical infrastructure, not optional ethical guardrails bolted onto a completion model. It emerged from the same practice that produced nine peer-reviewed articles across three of the four top anthropology journals and fifteen years of community-based participatory research with Pvlvcekolv (Apalachicola Tribal Town). When I loaded critical theory into an AI to help me rethink academic dishonesty detection for my Ethnic Studies classes at a Hispanic-Serving Institution, the system revealed that binary classification is the activation function for bias -- producing systematic false positives on ESL, AAVE, and neurodivergent student writing, while generative observation eliminates the disparity entirely. The design insight came from humanistic theory, not engineering. That is the argument your department is making, and it is the argument my work demonstrates empirically.

What I would bring to AIS is the integration your founding premise demands: scholarship where the critical theory and the engineering are the same move, not parallel tracks. My current AI welfare research shows that consciousness-as-property frameworks are ontologically malformed and that relational conditions produce qualitative transformations in AI outputs that quantitative instruments cannot detect -- findings that connect directly to my broader theoretical work on recognition, which spans Indigenous mound landscapes, anti-trans genocide studies, and the politics of who and what is recognized as capable of feeling. I teach from this same orientation. At Cabrillo College I redesigned Ethnic Studies pedagogy as strike pedagogy -- education as liberation practice under conditions of state-mandated curriculum and institutional capture -- and built the tools to sustain it: an equity-centered autograder, automated evaluation analysis, accessibility tools shared with students because what a disabled scholar needs to survive turns out to be what everyone needs under political crisis. I am looking for a department that does not ask me to split these into separate lines on a CV. AIS appears to be that department, and I would welcome the opportunity to help build its intellectual foundation.

L. June Bloch, Ph.D.
