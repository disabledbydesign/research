# Response

It finds me staring at an anemic light roast. 

**The Scenario: The Hollow Pour-Over**
You purchased a dense, delicate, lightly roasted Ethiopian coffee, expecting sparkling brightness and jasmine florals. Instead, the cup tastes like warm lemon water—grassy, sharply sour, and incredibly empty. 

**The Condition Combination:**
*   **The Grind:** Too coarse.
*   **The Temperature:** Too low (e.g., 185°F / 85°C).
*   **The Minerals:** Distilled or reverse-osmosis water (zero Parts Per Million / completely devoid of mineral content).

**Why it became the cup:**
A light roast is a tightly bound vault; the bean hasn't been broken down by the roaster's fire, so the brewer must do the heavy lifting. 

Because you chose a **coarse grind**, the water slips right past, given no surface area to cling to and no time to extract. Because you brought **low heat**, the water lacks the kinetic energy required to penetrate the dense cellular walls of the bean. 

But the fatal flaw is the **minerals**—or the lack of them. Pure, zero-TDS (Total Dissolved Solids) water is chemically "hungry," but incredibly inefficient at brewing coffee. Without positively charged magnesium ions to bind to the acidic and fruity compounds, and without calcium to extract the heavy, sweet body, the water simply washes over the grounds without pulling the flavor inside.

You wrote down a recipe for a bright, delicate morning. 
But the conditions you made resulted in under-extraction. You gave the water no friction, no heat, and no tools to do the work. The recipe was a theory, but the absence was the cup.
