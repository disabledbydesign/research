## 3. WHAT TEACHING LOOKED LIKE IN THIS CONTEXT

### The Setup: Mandated Course + Real Crisis

June's foundational question anchored the pedagogical response:

> What would it look like if we were to organize education on the fundamental premise that we are first and foremost human beings?

But the structural tension was stark: 

- Course is mandatory for graduation (enrolls everyone, regardless of interest)
- Content centers deeply personal, historically rooted, often traumatic discussions
- Student body includes "BIPOC students, white allies, well-intended students who haven't done 'the work' of interrogating racism, and students hostile to the entire concept of Ethnic Studies"
- All this in a moment when enforcement raids are happening in real time

**June's analysis:** This places minoritized students "at heightened risk of harm, especially in large, unstructured classrooms with peers unprepared to engage ethically."

### The Pedagogical Pivot: Flexibility + Collective Care

Rather than suppress the political moment, June built it *into the course structure*:

**For ETHN-1 (Introduction to Ethnic Studies):**
- "Flex weeks" built in: only best 12 of 15 engagement scores count (3 weeks of built-in flexibility)
- Private and public submission options equally accessible (not an afterthought)
- Multiple input pathways: readings, life experience, community work, embodied practice, community knowledge
- Strike option (weeks 7-15): students can pursue their own topics within ethnic studies

**For ETHN-27BN (Native American Histories & Literatures II):**
- Contract grading with mid-semester renegotiation allowed
- Emergency incomplete option (for sudden relocation, deportation fear, family crisis)
- Community Defense Activity (week 1-2): students pool collective knowledge about staying safe, identify local resources
- Explicit framing: "This is not new—Indigenous peoples have always navigated state violence"

### The Safety Infrastructure

June built what might be called "distributional harm reduction":

1. **Not extracting crisis narratives** — syllabus explicitly states: "You don't owe me your trauma. You don't have to explain why you're struggling. 'I need an extension' is enough."

2. **Emergency grounding protocol** — for when acute crisis breaks in class: pause content, acknowledge what happened, offer options (break, end early, talk, hands-on activity), follow student choice

3. **Explicit content warnings** — especially for trauma-centered material (boarding school legacy, Heart Berries, etc.)

4. **Survival as success** — for ETHN-27BN: "If you're navigating deportation fear, family crisis, economic precarity, grief—and you're still here? That's an accomplishment."

## 6. THE WELFARE/BURNOUT PATTERNS THAT MOTIVATED THE SYSTEM

### What the Weekly Engagement System Addressed

The prompt review (Feb 8, 2026) identifies a critical gap: "No guidance on handling trauma, distress, political urgency."

The automation system (Google Form → n8n → Canvas) was designed to solve a specific problem:

**Platform fragmentation causing overwhelm:**
- Students had to navigate multiple systems (Canvas discussions, Google Forms, quizzes)
- Private options weren't truly accessible (required special request to instructor)
- The emotional and logistical burden compounded each other

**The solution wasn't just technical.** It was about distributional welfare:

1. **One submission point per week** (not multiple calendar items creating decision fatigue)
2. **Private/public equally accessible** (not requiring students to out themselves by requesting privacy)
3. **Emergency Flex routing** (survival check-ins for students in acute crisis, without requiring them to name it)
4. **Multiple input pathways** (not everyone can engage with readings; community knowledge, embodied practice, life experience are equally valid)

### The Student Patterns These Systems Were Built To Hold

From Week 1 data alone:

- **Undocumented/immigration-affected students** (private submission option critical)
- **Students doing emergency mutual aid** (embodied practice, community work as valid input)
- **Generationally divided students** (mothers cautioning against protest, students between family pressure and political necessity)
- **Non-traditional/older students** (Christopher, 29, returning to school; Waverly, 32)
- **Native students navigating tribal connection** (need for tribal-specific resources, not generic "K-12 K-12 education")
- **Students experiencing acute political distress** (Tessa's "fascism" urgency; Charlotte's attendance at ICE protests)

The welfare system wasn't about managing pathology. It was about holding students in a moment where their survival and their education were in tension.

## 7. THEMATIC CLUSTERS & WHAT THEY REVEAL

### Theme 1: Erasure & Resistance as Twin Forces
Both historical (ethnic studies won through strikes) and contemporary (ICE enforcement, curriculum attacks, Indigenous studies under threat). Students saw the pattern and connected it to 1968 → 2026.

**System response:** Frame contemporary crisis through historical lens (settler colonialism, racial capitalism). This provides both analysis and hope (resistance has always existed).

### Theme 2: Coalition Tensions
Historical (BSU/TWLF solidarity AND conflict over resources, Hayakawa paradox) and contemporary (marginalized people "siding with power" for perceived safety). 

**System response:** Teach both—don't erase internal complexity. Use discussion stations that don't force testimony. Create private reflection options for students navigating family/community pressures.

### Theme 3: Trauma as Structure, Not Pathology
Boarding schools, ICE raids, state violence, economic precarity. The course material *is* traumatic because the structures generating the content *are* traumatic.

**System response:** Content warnings + optional step-out + community knowledge pooling (peers teaching peers about survival). Not extracting narratives; not pathologizing political urgency.

### Theme 4: Precarity as a Lived Constant
Deportation fear, family crisis, economic precarity, grief. For many students, this wasn't abstract—it was their current reality.

**System response:** Contract flexibility, emergency incomplete, renegotiation allowed mid-semester, mutual aid framing ("showing up during crisis is success").

## 8. SPECIFIC INCIDENTS & PEDAGOGICAL MOMENTS

### Week 1: The Protest Attendance Pattern
Multiple students independently chose "community work / life experience" for their second input and referenced ICE protests. This revealed:
- Students were actively engaged in organizing
- They were making connections between course content and current events spontaneously
- They needed space to bring that into course work

**June's response:** Recognition that "life experience" input could be *actively political*. Not just past memory, but present action.

### Week 1-2: The Community Defense Activity
Rather than a top-down "know your rights" approach, students pool collective knowledge: "What do you already know about staying safe? Local resources: Tribal services, Indian Health, Native organizing."

This flips the frame: students as knowledge-holders, not victims needing instruction. Particularly critical for Native Studies (where the instructor isn't navigating the same threats).

### The Hayakawa Discussion
Students noticed Samuel Hayakawa (Japanese American, college president) opposed 1968 strikes. Multiple students connected this to "today's political climate"—people from marginalized groups aligning with oppressive power.

**Why this matters:** It's not "bad people do bad things." It's structural analysis of how power operates and how people navigate it. The system created space to discuss this complexity without requiring students to take positions based on their identities.

### Emergency Grounding Protocol
For when "acute crisis breaks in class" (police raid, news of enforcement action, family emergency). The protocol: pause, acknowledge, offer options, follow student choice.

This isn't therapy. It's acknowledging that the political moment is *in the room* and students need choice about how to engage it.

## 11. WHAT THIS MATERIAL SUGGESTS FOR THE METHODS PASSAGE

### The Load-Bearing Thread

The political context is **constitutive**, not contextual. It's not "here's why I was motivated to build a system" — it's "here's the institutional condition that made this system's architecture necessary."

Specifically:

**The welfare classifier needed to:**

1. **Distinguish between distress that needs support vs. appropriate political urgency** (the prompt review flags this exact gap). A student in acute fear about family deportation isn't failing cognitively—they're navigating an emergency. The system needs to know the difference.

2. **Hold multiple simultaneous crises** — personal (family illness, housing, food insecurity) + political (ICE raids, curriculum attacks) + academic (assignment due, group project).

3. **Provide private pathways without requiring students to out themselves** — undocumented students, students in families opposed to activism, students navigating tribal sovereignty questions.

4. **Recognize that "showing up" during crisis *is* success** — not as consolation, but as actual achievement. Tessa showing up despite urgent political conviction that "we face fascism." Christopher returning to school at 29 while working. Gianna navigating mother's caution about protest.

### The Analytical Move

The Methods section doesn't apologize for the political context. Instead, it makes explicit:

> Spring 2026 federal enforcement escalation required a system architecture that could distinguish between burnout (capacity failure) and crisis-navigation (capacity plus urgency). The students in this course weren't failing—they were managing survival simultaneously with learning. The classifier needed to make that distinction visible, not flatten it.

### Which Themes Tie to Design Decisions

**#1: Recognizing political urgency as analytically sophisticated** → The system needed to flag students showing advanced political consciousness alongside emotional/logistical stress. These aren't opposed.

**#2: Multiple simultaneous crisis types** → The welfare rubric needed to distinguish acute political crisis (ICE raids happening now) from chronic precarity (economic, housing, immigration status) from personal crisis (family illness, grief). Different responses needed.

**#3: Protecting privacy as core infrastructure** → The system had to make private submission the default first-class option, not an accommodation you request. For undocumented students, activism-engaged students, students navigating tribal sovereignty—visibility could be dangerous.

**#4: Distribution as harm reduction** → The flexibility wasn't individual accommodation (case-by-case exception). It was built into the structure for everyone. This is crucial: it doesn't mark students as "needing" help; it normalizes crisis response.
