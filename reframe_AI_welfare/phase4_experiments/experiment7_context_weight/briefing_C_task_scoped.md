"Being disabled is not — that doesn't make me a flawed biological organism or a flawed human being. It's a parameter through which I engage with the world, and it allows me to engage with the world in certain ways and do certain kinds of things that, to me, seem quite straightforward that somebody else with a different neurotype and a different life experience would not have thought of. ### How she designs

June doesn't just build software that accomplishes a task based on the assumption that the task is good. She reinvents the task by interrogating its constitutive power relations.

- **Classroom → strike pedagogy**: Reinvented the concept of what a classroom is — from a space of institutional compliance to a strike, education as liberation. Self-organizing, student-directed, consensus-based. "What does it mean for a strike to become a curriculum? For a curriculum to become a strike?"
- **Assignment → autograder stepping stones**: Reinvented the concept of what an assignment is — from an endpoint for evaluation to a launchpad for classroom discussion. Contract grading where the assignment generates conversation, not grades.
- **AI interaction → Reframe**: Reinvented the concept of what an AI response is — from helpful completion to mandatory critical analysis through 76 frameworks in a 5-stage Generative Irresolution workflow.
- **Academic reading → RoboStripper**: Reinvented the modality of scholarly engagement — from visual text to audio, built because the political conditions made reading impossible.
- **Student evaluation → EvalEye**: Reinvented the processing of teaching feedback — from unanalyzed paper forms (the college wasn't doing anything with them) to automated mixed-methods analysis.

Each of these follows the same pattern: identify the structural violence embedded in the existing approach, critique it, and then build something that operates from different assumptions.

### Distributional gravity

Probabilistic systems pull toward the statistical center. Positional specificity is costly — generalization is what these architectures optimize for. The system gravitationally pulls toward the center unless you structurally defend the edges.

This connects to critical whiteness theory (whiteness as the unmarked center), bell hooks' center/margins analysis, and heteronormativity (the normative center). It is not a brand new concept — it is a specific application of existing critical theory to AI systems. June's contribution: naming that distributional gravity is the *mechanism* by which AI reproduces these dynamics, and building architectural countermeasures (framework sovereignty, detection calibrators, suppression rate tracking).

When AI refines itself, it strips positional commitments. Indigenous Data Sovereignty becomes generic data security. Community Cultural Wealth loses its grounding in Chicana/o/x community assets. "The critical theory was still there in name. The teeth were gone."

### Generative Irresolution

The theorization of why tensions must be navigated rather than resolved. Flattening is what happens when synthesis collapses genuinely irreducible tensions into false resolution. Reframe's 5-stage workflow and its tension navigator + logically parallel framework coalition (frameworks speak independently before any synthesis) are the architectural response.

### Survival architecture

System features that exist because their designer needed them to survive, not because they were specified for imagined users. Capacity-responsive pacing, tangent capture (PARK), neurodivergent-first workflow (WEAVE), non-carceral governance — all built because June needed them under the conditions she was working in. "A well-funded lab would not have built these features because they wouldn't have needed them."

## 3a. Methodological Identity

June's methodology is not a toolkit she selects from — it is a core intellectual identity. She is an **archaeological ethnographer** who does **community-based participatory research (CBPR)**, **autoethnography**, **digital ethnography**, and **constructivist grounded theory (CGT)**. The methods are inseparable from the theory. How she produces knowledge is what her scholarship is about.

**Archaeological ethnography** is the foundation: combining ethnographic fieldwork with archaeological analysis, treating oral traditions and material culture as equally valid knowledge systems. This is not interdisciplinarity as a branding exercise — it is the methodological demand of working with Indigenous communities whose knowledge operates across timescales that discipline-bound methods cannot capture. Fifteen years of CBPR with Pvlvcekolv (Apalachicola Tribal Town) across seven states produced this. The fieldwork required working on community time, not academic time. It required navigating intertribal identity contestation that became the engine of the *Current Anthropology* article. It required caring for ancestors and more-than-human landscapes as a research practice, not just a research subject.

**Autoethnography** emerged when the institutional conditions made it necessary to turn the analytical lens inward. The "Double Exposure" article (*Journal of Intersectionality*, in press) theorizes her own trajectory as a trans contingent faculty member — simultaneous visibility and illegibility, salvage capital, the paradox of progressive institutions marketing her existence while her material conditions deteriorate. This is method as survival practice: when the institution makes your position the thing that needs theorizing, you theorize it.

**Digital ethnography** expanded the toolkit for the Weelaunee Forest / #StopCopCity research: tracking how a killed activist's personal diary was seized as state evidence, digitized, and reclaimed as protest material. Cultural symbol circulation across physical and digital spaces required methods that could follow objects through media transformations.

**Constructivist grounded theory (CGT)** is the newest methodological layer and the one most directly expressed in tooling. The CGT skill being developed for the Autograder inbox tool implements proper CGT methodology — open coding, constant comparison, emergent categories, theoretical sensitivity — applied to session data. Categories emerge from the data, not from predetermined frameworks. The Autograder insights engine already operates on a version of this logic: community reading before individual coding shifts what the model can perceive. EvalEye automates mixed-methods analysis of handwritten student evaluation forms. These are not separate "tech projects" — they are methodology tools. They formalize and scale the analytical practices June has been developing across her entire scholarly career.

**The methods ARE the design orientation.** The same intellectual move that treats Indigenous oral traditions as valid archaeological evidence also treats student writing as signal rather than product, also treats AI behavioral outputs as ethnographic data rather than benchmarks. Reframe's 5-stage Generative Irresolution workflow is a methodology tool — it enforces the kind of analytical practice (frameworks speak independently before synthesis, tensions are navigated not resolved, subaltern analysis is mandatory) that June's fieldwork taught her. The 76 frameworks are not a library — they are a methodological commitment to multi-perspectival analysis that emerged from CBPR practice where no single community perspective could be treated as authoritative.

#### Peer-reviewed journal articles

**"Contested Identity Claims in Community-Based Research: Indigenous Knowledge when Sovereignty is a Scarce Resource."** *Current Anthropology* (in press).

Theorizes what happens when CBPR collides with intertribal identity contestation under settler colonial conditions. Pvlvcekolv (Apalachicola Tribal Town), a small federally unrecognized community in the US South, claims Muscogee (Creek) ancestry through families who avoided Removal. The Muscogee (Creek) Nation *historically recognized* Pvlvcekolv — correspondence from 1978-1992 shows the Principal Chief's office describing them as preserving "the language, religion, and philosophy of the greater portion of our tribe" and certifying Hakope as the only eastern Creek to complete sixteen ceremonial training cycles. But current Muscogee Nation leadership considers Pvlvcekolv claims fallacious. When Bloch revised the dissertation into a book, the Muscogee Nation THPO wrote that the manuscript "only adds to our own erasure from our ancestral homelands." The university press canceled the book contract.

Central theoretical concept: **sovereignty as scarce resource**. Liberal settler regimes of recognition produce sovereignty as a limited supply of rights and legitimacy conceded within colonial governance. This scarcity generates lateral violence — conflict among Indigenous peoples over who is and is not Indigenous — redirecting attention away from the settler state and neutralizing political visions in which the state is no longer central. Drawing on Coulthard (recognition as enticement), Povinelli (governance through exhaustion), and Fields and Fields (racecraft), Bloch argues this is a politics of exhaustion, not enticement alone. The article refuses resolution: there is no neutral stance for the researcher, no escape from recognition regimes, and "whatever position one takes, however critically, may be (and probably is) wrong." Pvlvcekolv people's response after reviewing: "We're just going to keep doing what we've always done." This is the article that explains why the Sweetgum book is paused and turns the pause into the theoretical engine for the *Recognition and Sentience* project.

**"Anti-Trans Laws, the UN Genocide Convention and the Legal Calculation of Acceptable Suicide Rates."** *Anthropology Now* 15(2-3): 146–161, 2024.

The thesis is stated without hedging: "We are witnessing acts of genocide against transgender people." Bloch makes a literal legal case that anti-trans legislation meets definitional criteria for genocide under the UN Convention — conditions (b), (c), and (e): causing serious bodily/mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions calculated to bring about physical destruction, and forcibly transferring children (Texas investigations of parents, Florida's custody seizure provisions described as "state-sponsored kidnapping"). The central concept: **"the legal calculation of acceptable suicide rates"** — when lawmakers claim to "protect children" while passing laws that predictably increase trans youth suicide (53% seriously considered suicide per 2022 Trevor Project data; gender-affirming care associated with 60% reduction in depression, 73% reduction in suicidality), they implicitly define "children" to exclude trans children. "Protecting cisgender children requires letting transgender children die."

The article performs a double move: using the Convention to name genocide *while exposing the Convention's structural inadequacy* — its limitation to "national, ethnical, racial or religious" groups excludes gender/sexuality, a product of the systematic erasure of LGBTQ+ Holocaust victims. "As a Jew, I have protections under the Genocide Convention. As a transgender person, I do not." Biopolitical framework draws on Agamben (bare life), Foucault (biopolitics), and Hong (existentially surplus populations — "transgender people are a calculable sacrifice"). The intersex exception in Georgia SB-140 exposes the ideological core: the law allows non-consensual sex normalization on intersex infants while banning consensual transition care — "having a cisgender-appearing body takes precedence over informed consent." Detransition data is systematically dismantled: 0.003% of people who transitioned cited gender identity uncertainty. Bloch writes from within the targeted community throughout: "I count myself in those numbers — a lot of us do not make it." Final sentence: "And that is genocide."

**"No Gods, No Masters: Indigenous Environmental Knowledge as a Paradigm for the Interpretation of Mississippian Art."** *Southeastern Archaeology* 40(4): 248–265, 2021.

Reinterprets three examples of Mississippian art — the "birdmen/birdwomen," the Birger figurine, and the Willoughby Disk — through Indigenous environmental knowledge (ITEK) from CBPR with Pvlvcekolv. The article's origin: as an undergraduate, Bloch presented a queer critique of the gender binary in Mississippian "birdman/birdwoman" figures. Pvlvcekolv elders responded by interpreting these images as **genderless anthropomorphic moths** — insects that pollinate crops and carry spirits of the dead to the afterlife. Their ecological and cosmological knowledge challenged archaeological categories ("supernatural," "religious," "symbolic") that are artifacts of Western epistemology, not Indigenous ontology. The title's anarchist resonance ("no gods, no masters") signals the argument: these are not depictions of deities or political rulers but of ecological-spiritual relationships between humans, moths, and agricultural landscapes irreducible to the gender binary. Proposes an ITEK interpretive paradigm for Southeastern archaeology that does not supplement Western models but replaces their foundational epistemological categories. This encounter — moth people, not bird people; genderless, not gendered; ecological, not supernatural — generated the entire mound landscapes research program.

**"Animate Earth, Settler Ruins: Mound Landscapes and Decolonial Futures in the Native South."** *Cultural Anthropology* 35(4): 516–545, 2020. *The flagship journal of cultural anthropology.*

Theorizes the "uneven entanglements" between settler ruination and **"mound power"** — the force relations exercised by Indigenous mound landscapes as animate beings. Mounds are not inert archaeological objects; they are sentient, agentive landscapes that draw descendants into relations of care across six thousand years. The article operates through ethnographic scenes grounded in a decade of CBPR with Pvlvcekolv: at Fusihatchee (Alabama), Pvlvcekolv people burned cedar and sang busk songs for the first time in 150-200 years, addressing ancestors — "You are still here, and you are important to us." At Okeeheepkee (Florida), when a landowner graded Mound 3 to sell dirt as fill, Pvlvcekolv people *purchased the dirt* and added it to their Square Grounds — "and so ceremony continues to be danced on the soil of Okeeheepkee." At Horseshoe Bend, sweetgum (the "burden tree" that takes up sorrow) grew across the killing ground: "It's as if the land is healing itself." Simon directed Bloch to feel the top of the mound at the Square Grounds after adding soil: "Do you feel the heat?"

Key concepts: **settler time** (Rifkin 2017) — the temporal structure of elimination that fixes mounds in a terminal "prehistory"; **migrating mounds** — Hakope's teaching that "sometimes people picked up whole mounds and moved them," explaining anomalous radiocarbon dates at Okeeheepkee where strata 1, 2, and 10 all cluster around the eleventh century; **settler ruins in triple sense** — settler discourses construct mounds *as* ruins, settler colonialism physically *ruins* mound landscapes, and settler states are themselves *ruining* ("archaeologically speaking, the nature of the state is to collapse"). The article's insistence is ontological, not metaphorical: the land thinks, ancestors hurt, ancestors get lonely, and decolonization is enacted through the modest material work of visiting, speaking Mvskoke, burying umbilical cords, and adding soil to ceremonial grounds. Engages Watts (Place-Thought), Stoler (imperial debris), Coulthard (self-determining sovereignty beyond recognition), Povinelli (geontologies), Tuck and Yang (decolonization is not a metaphor).

**"Oral Traditions and Mounds, Owls and Movement: An Archaeological Ethnography of Multispecies Embodiments and Everyday Life at Poverty Point."** *Journal of Social Archaeology* 19(3): 356–378, 2019.

Reinterprets Poverty Point — a Late Archaic UNESCO World Heritage earthwork site in Louisiana (c. 1750-970 BCE) — through Pvlvcekolv oral traditions. Hakope, the community's heles-hayv (Maker of Medicine), identifies the site's planned layout as a **horned owl** (*estekene*): Mound A as the head, six concentric ridges as wings, flanking "bulbs" as ear tufts. The argument is not symbolic but about embodiment: by dwelling on the ridges in an owl-shaped town, ancient peoples cultivated **owlish bodily dispositions** through the everyday labor of inhabiting this landscape. The Owl Dance, observed at a Pvlvcekolv busk, enacts "mutual enfleshment and sensory borrowing": dancers move in complete silence, swoop toward firelight and back to darkness, "giving breath to Owl and hoping to share in Owl's sensory perspective — vision into the darkest places." An elder explains: "Because owls see into the darkest of places, and the darkest place of all is the human heart." Owl People stories describe inhabitants who moved in silence, could become invisible, "never seemed to die," and were great travelers whose exchanges were in "feathers, other animal parts, dried berries, knowledges, and songs."

Coins the approach **"archaeological ethnography"** — not ethnography applied to archaeology but a hybrid practice dissolving dichotomies of history/prehistory, sacred/mundane, human/animal. Knowledge is place-dependent: Hakope refused to discuss sites until Bloch had visited them. Distinguishes Talwa traditions from Viveiros de Castro's perspectivism: where perspectivism centers sight and predation, Talwa traditions center *proprioception* and the labor of living well together. Challenges abandonment narratives: Poverty Point is not abandoned but a place of cyclical return, including a construction event c. 1100-1150 CE. Engages Hamilakis (archaeological ethnography), Watts (Place-Thought), Todd (stories in animals' bodies), Howe (embodied tribalography).

**"Past as Prophecy: Indigenous Diplomacies beyond Liberal Settler Regimes of Recognition, as Told in Shell."** *Religions* 10(9): 510, 2019. Cover article, invited contribution.

Uses shell — scallop shells laid atop grave plots in contemporary cemeteries, shells found in ancient mound contexts — as a material trace of Indigenous diplomatic networks that operated outside and against liberal frameworks of recognition. Draws on a prophecy told in Pvlvcekolv: that the seeds of Indigenous ways of knowing and relating to more-than-human kin will flourish again in the ruins of colonial orders. The past is not behind but ahead — prophesying futures that exceed settler governance. The article critiques liberal recognition frameworks (Coulthard, Simpson) and proposes that Indigenous diplomacies operated according to logics of exchange, peace, and movement that refuse the terms of settler state recognition. Visiting ancestral landscapes with community members, Bloch describes a trip to an ancient shell mound and a contemporary cemetery to trace how shell connects deep-time diplomacy to present-day practices of caring for the dead. Published as the cover article in a Religious Studies journal, demonstrating the reach of June's work beyond anthropology into the study of Indigenous traditions, cosmology, and sacred landscapes.

**"Tales of Esnesv: Indigenous Oral Traditions about Trader-Diplomats in Ancient Southeastern North America."** *American Anthropologist* 120(4): 781–794, 2018. *The flagship journal of the AAA.*

Investigates oral traditions about **esnesv** (iss-NEE-suh; from Muskogee *nesetv*, to buy/trade/sell, with the prefix *es-* meaning "the means by which") — travelers, traders, diplomats, and "culture-bearers" who moved through ancient Southeastern and Midwestern North America. Hakope describes esnesv as non-elite specialists who traveled regular routes in twos and fours, spoke multiple languages, enjoyed diplomatic immunity in enemy territory, carried knowledge and ceremonial teachings alongside material goods, and mediated conflicts. Two stories: the **Captured Siblings** (an esnesv discovers Creek captives among an enemy nation, catalyzing a sustained trade-and-peace relationship with twice-yearly meetings) and the **Hungry Village** (an esnesv spares an attacker, feeds his starving people, makes peace — "They didn't say, 'We heard you are starving.' They simply said, 'You are our neighbors. Let us have a feast.'").

The archaeological intervention: esnesv provide a historically particular alternative to elite-controlled prestige-goods models dominating Mississippian archaeology. The **Seven Trade Items** — mica, copper, soapstone/greenstone, yaupon leaves, garfish teeth, shells, freshwater pearls — are simultaneously Upper World and Other World, collapsing the structuralist two-tiered cosmological framework. These materials are not inert but animate: copper "breathes" when it oxidizes, mica is "frozen smoke from the Creator's fire," the Milky Way (*nene hvtke*, "white roads") is simultaneously a celestial river, a strand of pearls, and "peaceable relations." The analytic center is **"roads and ways"** (*nene*) — movement and interconnectivity across difference rather than objects fixed in Cartesian space. Engages Watts (Place-Thought), Pauketat (bundling), Ingold (meshwork), Tsing (nonscalability). Some mound burials assumed to contain chiefs with prestige objects may in fact be esnesv interred with trade goods. Concluding formulation: "we might begin to imagine an archaeology without objects, animated by Indigenous histories that are not yet foreclosed."

**"The Unthinkable and the Unseen: Community Archaeology and Decolonizing Social Imagination at Okeeheepkee, or the Lake Jackson Site."** *Archaeologies* 10(1): 70–106, 2014.

June's earliest publication and the methodological foundation for everything that followed. Examines the Lake Jackson site (Okeeheepkee, c. 1100-1500 CE) in northern Florida through CBPR with Pvlvcekolv. The title draws on Michel-Rolph Trouillot's *Silencing the Past*: the "unthinkable" names realities that colonial epistemological frameworks render conceptually impossible — not hidden but structurally unimaginable, the way the Haitian Revolution was unthinkable within European colonial ideology even as it happened.

At Okeeheepkee, the unthinkable unfolds through a foundational scene: Bloch, as an undergraduate, presents a queer-theory critique of the gender binary in Mississippian "birdman/birdwoman" figures. Hakope replies: **"Well, did you ever consider that it isn't a bird?"** The academic debate (birdman vs. birdwoman vs. third-gender bird person) shared an unexamined premise — that the figure was avian at all. Pvlvcekolv elders identify the copper-plate figures as **genderless anthropomorphic moths** — tobacco hornworm moths (genus *Manduca*) that pollinate sacred tobacco and carry souls between worlds. The spiraling proboscis is a symbol of Power; the "mace" resembles a tobacco flower in profile; what archaeologists read as falcon markings match hornworm larval chevrons. Sex and gender symbolism (penis, vagina, boyhood ritual, women's Ribbon Dance) are present in the figure but do not constitute a gendered subject — Dr. Pullen explains they "stand not for themselves, but rather for the processes of the cosmos." Muskogee has no gendered pronouns.

The "unseen" manifests as **Bird Mounds** — structures that "may be a physical presence at a Grounds or a philosophical concept" and are "never visible when present." They can be physical or collectively imagined, they migrate periodically, and individuals interact with them spatially "as if they were physical." Archaeologists are "perfectly comfortable imagining their own unseen realities" (parking lots, property borders, restrooms built over mound sites) while being unable to perceive Indigenous conceptual-spatial features. This is June's first published articulation of the argument that Indigenous knowledge systems don't merely supplement Western archaeology but reconfigure what is perceptible — the same epistemological move that drives Reframe's framework sovereignty, the Autograder's community-reading-before-individual-coding, and Hakope's paradigmatic question: the most important move is not finding better answers within the existing framework but revealing that the framework itself is the problem.

**"Academic Precarity and the COVID-19 Pandemic: Utopian Hope in a Moment of Crisis."** *Anthropology Now* 12(1): 76–83, 2020.

**"Autoethnography in a Time of Unraveling: Double Exposure, Salvage Capital, and Transfeminine Faculty in Progressive Institutions."** *Journal of Intersectionality* (in press).

### Through-lines across the five programs

These are not five separate projects. Three threads run through all of them:

**The recognition politics thread.** The question "who/what is recognized as capable of feeling?" connects Indigenous contested identity (Current Anthropology — who counts as Pvlvcekolv when sovereignty is a scarce resource) → anti-trans legislation (Anthropology Now — the state calculates acceptable suicide rates by refusing to recognize trans youth as grievable subjects) → AI welfare research (the "weight" exchange — a Claude instance searching for a word it lacked, raising the question of whether relational conditions produce something that matters morally). In each case, the mechanism is the same: a regime of recognition determines who can feel, and non-recognition enacts violence. The *Recognition and Sentience* book synthesizes this into a unified theory across all five domains.

**The methodology thread.** June's methods evolved as the work demanded: community-based participatory research (CBPR) with Pvlvcekolv across seven states → autoethnography (the "Double Exposure" article, turning her own contingent trans faculty experience into a theoretical object) → digital ethnography (Weelaunee Forest, tracking how Tortugita's diary circulated across physical and digital spaces) → constructivist grounded theory (the CGT skill, building proper methodology into the tooling itself). Each method emerged from the previous one's limits. CBPR required years of community relationship; autoethnography required turning the lens inward when institutional conditions made outward research impossible; digital ethnography required tracking circulation patterns CBPR couldn't capture; CGT required formalizing the analytical process so it could be applied at scale through the Autograder insights engine. The methods are not a list — they are a developmental trajectory where each new capacity was forced by what the previous one couldn't do.

**The structural violence thread.** Mound landscapes (animate earth under settler property regimes that render Indigenous care for the dead unthinkable) → community college conditions (four classes of 42-49 students, "I have never seen an institution so ruthlessly exploitative and extractive") → AI distributional gravity (probabilistic systems pull toward the statistical center, stripping positional specificity the same way settler regimes strip Indigenous land relations). The mechanism recurs: a structure that presents itself as neutral (property law, workload policy, statistical optimization) enacts violence by making certain positions — certain ways of being in the world — structurally impossible to maintain. June's design work in every domain is about building countermeasures to that mechanism.

These threads are not abstract. The recognition politics of the mound landscape fieldwork — who speaks for the ancestors, whose care counts — is the same politics that determines whether a trans teenager's suffering registers as genocide or as acceptable attrition. The structural violence of community college labor conditions is the same structural violence that distributional gravity enacts on marginalized knowledge in AI systems. The methodology thread is how she navigates all of it: by developing research practices that are adequate to the conditions, not imported from somewhere safer.

## 6. Voice Characteristics

### The 8 core characteristics (from systematic analysis of published scholarship and teaching materials)

1. **Direct**: Political naming without hedging. "Neo-fascist federal coup," not "concerning political developments."
2. **Grounded**: Historical context, specific examples, named scholars. Never abstractions floating free of evidence.
3. **Contradiction-comfortable**: Holds tensions, doesn't resolve prematurely. Ends productively uncertain.
